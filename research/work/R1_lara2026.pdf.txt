Are You Sure This is What You Really Want? Conversational 
Recommender Systems for Enhancing Human Autonomy
Francisco Lara1  · Juan Ignacio del Valle1
Abstract
Recommender systems are widely used to help users navigate information overload 
in digital environments. While they are often portrayed as tools that enhance auton -
omy by optimizing choice and personalizing content, this article argues that many 
current recommender systems designs in fact undermine user autonomy. Drawing 
on a conception of autonomy in which the formation and configuration of prefer -
ences, particularly higher-level desires in the Frankfurtian sense, play a prominent 
role, we examine how dominant recommendation techniques shape users’ desires 
and, to a greater extent, their identities. We first analyze what we call conjectural 
recommenders, systems that infer preferences from behavioral data such as clicks 
or viewing histories. These systems conflate observed choices with genuine prefer -
ences, reinforcing first-order desires, narrowing the diversity of recommendations, 
and trapping users in homogeneous digital environments that impede identity de -
velopment and degrade deliberative capacities. We then assess interrogative recom -
menders, which incorporate explicit user feedback as a form of positive friction in 
the user interface. Although these recommenders prompt users to articulate evalua -
tions, we argue that they remain insufficient for capturing second-order desires and 
raise new design challenges concerning paternalism and usability. In response, we 
propose the Socratic conversational recommender system, a model that combines 
conversational recommendation with a second layer of Socratic questioning aimed 
at eliciting users’ metapreferences. Rather than prescribing substantive values, 
the system guides reflection through formal criteria such as coherence, empirical 
awareness, and cognitive pluralism. The objective is not for the system to discover 
users’ true preferences but to foster their reflective engagement with what they want 
to want. We conclude that embedding deliberative dialogue within recommender 
design offers a promising pathway for aligning algorithmic recommendation with 
the preservation and development of human autonomy.
Keywords Recommender systems · Autonomy · Conversational recommendation · 
Digital ethics · Ethic of recommendation · Socratic assistant

Introduction
Recommender systems (RS) are designed to facilitate users’ decision-making in cer-
tain internet services. They are systems composed of algorithms that aim to make 
suggestions about items that the user might like or find useful. Currently, these appli-
cations, after collecting and analyzing data about user preferences and about products 
and services on the internet, are capable of offering predictions about the possible 
rating that the user would give to certain items; ultimately indicating to the user the 
items supposedly best valued by them, in terms of interest or need (Burke et al., 1996; 
Burke, 1999; Resnick & Varian, 1997).
These RSs are widely used in many internet applications, particularly since the 
disruption of AI, in its Machine Learning variant, has substantially improved their 
performance. They are common tools in applications that help users facing informa-
tion overload in e-commerce, entertainment, education, or social networks. Thus, 
they suggest items that may interest users, such as certain information, news, music, 
videos and films, consumer goods, tourist destinations, or people seeking partners. 
Amazon, Netflix, and Spotify are well-known examples of applications that system-
atically offer these systems.
At first glance, this use of RSs appears highly relevant to users’ well-being and 
autonomy. To begin with, given the information overload we encounter when making 
online choices, these systems are highly effective tools for optimizing choice. Thanks 
to the internet, we currently have the advantage of being able to acquire information, 
goods, and services that can be of great interest to us, but that could remain a mere 
unreachable possibility if we did not have systems that filter that immense quantity 
of options presented to us and suggest what might interest us most. A human being 
would hardly have the capacity or time to adequately know all available items and 
rank them according to their preferences. The RS does this for us, enabling us to 
acquire what benefits us and what we would have had great difficulty obtaining on 
our own. But that is not all. By performing the search and selection of what is best 
for us on our behalf, the system would also allow us to direct the time and effort that 
this would have required toward other, more gratifying tasks or activities. It is evident 
that individual freedom is measured not only by a greater range of choices but also 
by the availability of time. Thus, if the system performs the task of choosing certain 
items for us, we have more time to use as we wish. Finally, it could also be added that 
the use of RS would never diminish our human autonomy, since there is always the 
option of not accepting the system’s recommendations.
This intuitive assessment that RSs, by default, would not only not diminish our 
autonomy and well-being, but could also increase them, is corroborated by an experi-
mental study concluding that interviewed subjects, internet users of algorithmic 
decision-making systems, highlight that, in general, with the use of such systems, in 
addition to never losing control, they increase their choice options and, thanks to the 
high degree of personalization in recommendations, their desires are better satisfied 
(Dogruel et al., 2022).
But reality is quite different, because autonomy is more than satisfying one’s 
desires. It is a complex phenomenon in which the formation and configuration of 
those very desires have a prominent place. In a basic sense, for desires to be truly

autonomous, they should result from a process independent of avoidable external 
influences; they should be conceivable as belonging to the individual who holds them 
and should respond, to some degree, to that individual’s rational capacity (Christman, 
2020)1. It would, therefore, be a questionably simplistic position to maintain that an 
RS promotes autonomy simply because it satisfies users’ desires. Thus, for example, 
there would be no advancement in terms of autonomy if the desires that are satisfied 
are the result of a surreptitious (manipulative) influence to have them, of circum -
stances that, due to extreme lack of options, condition adaptive preferences, or of a 
blind and compulsive addiction.
The literature on how RSs actually undermine autonomy is not very extensive, 
and what exists is highly fragmented. A few years ago, Milano et al. ( 2020, p. 2), 
in discussing the ethics of RSs in general, noted the scarcity of literature, citing the 
novelty of these technologies and the fact that independent researchers cannot access 
the internal operations of these algorithmic systems due to industrial and commercial 
secrecy. Currently, although RS technology is no longer so novel and there is greater 
knowledge of its design, the literature remains scarce, particularly regarding the 
implications of these systems for human autonomy. This is highly surprising given 
the importance of autonomy in individual and socio-political terms: it is a constitu -
tive trait of human beings, as well as an indispensable instrument for both personal 
well-being and development, and for the proper functioning of democratic systems.
Within this scarce existing literature, one part is devoted to studying the extent 
to which the emergence of RSs can facilitate political and commercial manipulation 
(Hildebrandt, 2022), especially through the use of hypernudging, that is, the person-
alization and dynamic reconfiguration of choice architectures based on user feedback 
and their anticipated preferences (Yeung, 2017). Other issues collateral to the con -
cern about manipulative threats to autonomy are also analyzed, such as the lack of 
transparency for users regarding the process of obtaining the recommendations they 
receive (Ricci et al., 2022; Herlocker et al., 2000) or the problems related to privacy 
during the collection of personal data used by these systems (Friedman et al., 2015; 
Koene et al., 2015).
These topics, however, fall outside the essential focus of this article. We will 
instead focus on the risks that RSs pose to autonomy, understood here as the forma -
tion of desires in accordance with the basic criteria of authenticity and the exercise 
of self-control and reflective capacity to act accordingly. Specifically, the objective 
of this article is twofold. Firstly, to demonstrate that the recommendation techniques 
used in the main types of RS designed to date endanger autonomy because they 
bypass and deteriorate the human skills necessary for the deliberative formation of 
preferences, which in turn constitutes a serious obstacle to developing the user’s 
personal identity. This, in particular, will be the subject of the following two sections. 
In the second section, we analyze these undesirable effects in versions of systems 
that base their recommendations on data about the user’s digital behavior—that is,

could be achieved through recent advances in conversational recommender propos -

to the RS domain the Socratic model of moral enhancement proposed in Lara and 
Deckers (2020) and developed in Lara ( 2021) for the design of virtual ethical assis -
tants. This model is best understood as one position within a longer-standing debate 
on AI-assisted moral enhancement. Well before these proposals, Klincewicz ( 2016) 
argued that AI could serve moral enhancement through an artificial moral reasoning 
engine that presents users with arguments grounded in first-order normative theo -
ries, while warning that the persuasive force of such a system may rest partly on the 
undue trust that people place in automated technologies. Klincewicz ( 2019) subse-
quently explored how artificial agents might nudge users toward practices of moral 
self-cultivation, making explicit the trade-off between effective guidance and respect 
for the user’s autonomy that any AI assistant engaged in dialogue must negotiate, 
together with the attendant risks of paternalism and manipulation. Alongside related 
proposals such as the “moral AI” of Savulescu and Maslen (2015) and the “artificial 
moral advisor” of Giubilini and Savulescu ( 2018), this literature frames the alterna -
tive that Lara and Deckers (2020) later operationalize: between an advisory model, in 
which the system supplies moral answers, and a Socratic model, in which it merely 
stimulates the user’s own deliberation. Our proposal takes the Socratic side of this 
debate; but, as we argue in section ”Socratic RS”, the challenges articulated in that 
earlier literature, remain pressing for any recommender built along these lines, and 
we address them directly there.
Autonomy and Conjectural Recommenders
The main threats to autonomy from the most widespread RSs today arise from the 
use of a basic approach to designing operations, whereby these systems predict which 
items the user is supposedly most likely to value. They are designed to record past 
or current user actions, such as clicks or viewing time on specific items, during their 
interactions with digital applications. Although several recommendation techniques 
can be derived from this form of algorithmic design, they can all be encompassed

under the title of “implicit feedback RS,” or, as we will call it, “conjectural recom -
mender” (CR).
Underlying this type of recommender is a twofold “conjecture” that makes them 
appear, in principle, highly efficient: on the one hand, the conjecture that if a certain 
item has pleased the user because of its content, the user should also like another item 
with similar content; and on the other hand, the conjecture that the user’s original 
tastes are evident from the very behavioral facts that support them—that is, from the 
fact that the user chose them.
However, the main problem with maintaining this scheme of feeding systems pri-
marily with behavioral data is that it ignores the gap between behavior and human 
preferences. Our choices, including those in the everyday use of digital applications, 
do not always align with our life plans, essential values, or personal development 
purposes; in short, choices do not always match what we would want to want. There-
fore, if the system continues to recommend similar content to us based on our past 
or present choices, it will ultimately end up reducing the field of options or oppor -
tunities so much (to those remaining on one side of the gap) that it cannot be said to 
recommend authentic preferences, since it will not count those that do not appear in 
our choice history (which are on the other side of the gap), and which often better 
determine what we really want.
In other words, CRs would hinder users’ capacity to form those second-order 
desires that respond to deeper purposes maintained over extended periods of time, 
and that, according to H. Frankfurt ( 1971) and G. Dworkin ( 1988), would charac -
terize genuine autonomy. The vast majority of RSs are based on a narrow form of 
human engagement, focused mainly on providing extreme fluidity in their use, exem-
plified by the one-click buy button, the autocomplete function in search engines, or 
the automatic video playback. This mainly leads to automation bias: by encouraging 
immediate reward, this design maximizes uncritical acceptance of proposed recom -
mendations, and hinders the pursuit of preferences through active wanting. This ren-
ders us highly passive and distances us from what is distinctly human, characterized 
by acting through rationalization and reflection on what one does and thereby shaping 
one’s preferences and determining one’s future (Krook & Blockx, 2023).
Furthermore, by narrowing perspective and repeatedly offering to satisfy imme -
diate desires, the only result is the reinforcement of an addictive tendency in us to 
always consume the same things. This weakens the user’s self-control capacity and 
usually leads them to a negative feeling about their own agency, with the consequent 
remorse for having dedicated so much time to the same thing, normally something 
very much in line with their most superficial preferences (Burr et al., 2018; de Vries, 
2010; Koene et al., 2015). W. Lu ( 2024) deepens this line of criticism of CRs by 
arguing that decisions made with personalized algorithms, unlike exclusively human 
ones, make it difficult for those decisions to be self-corrected. When making deci -
sions in accordance with such algorithms, one would dispense with the traditional 
correction mechanisms in the non-digital world that help make decisions less biased 
and therefore more autonomous: social interactions and rational deliberation. That 
is, because the decisions suggested by the systems are made in a closed environ -
ment, users would not be exposed to perspectives different from their own and the 
system’s—perspectives that could reveal those cognitive biases.

Another of the main criticisms of CRs is directed, above all, at the threats this tech-
nique poses to the free development of personal identity. On the one hand, because 
it causes the user to become “trapped in the past.” The system, by considering the 
user’s current behavior—for instance, more or less recent clicks or viewings—bases 
its recommendations on a snapshot of who the user has been or is now, rather than on 
who the user wants to become in the future. The user’s past desires are strongly rein-
forced by the CR, even when the user, through second-order desires, had been think-
ing for some time about changing their desires or, even, had begun at that moment a 
gradual change that, due to its limited history, does not end up being relevant in the 
profile the system has assigned to them. It is in this sense that, given the preponder -
ance of past preferences and the insistence on recommendations based on them, it 
could be said that the user is, in a way, trapped in time (Krook & Blockx, 2023).
On the other hand, this technique is also an impediment to personal development 
because it traps the user not only in the past but also in the same content. The CR 
technique renders the user’s digital environment highly homogeneous and immuta -
ble, thereby trapping them in a filter bubble that also inhibits personal and intellectual 
development (Nguyen et al., 2014; Bozdag & Van Den Hoven, 2015).
In short, the blocking of change initiatives in preferences and the consequent 
absence of new content place the user in an “identity inertia,” whereby the normative 
principles of the future self tend to be the same as those of the present self (Loi et 
al., 2020).
Within this category of CRs, we would also include collaborative filtering tech -
niques. In these cases, the aim is to generate recommendations by creating user 
profiles (profiling) and grouping them into similar clusters, which may be social or 
demographic categories such as age, gender, marital status, and so on (Yannam et al., 
2023).
This could be considered an advance insofar as it would somewhat broaden the 
range of opportunities and options for the user, since members with common group 
preferences or categories might also have distinct preferences that would end up being 
recommended to that user. However, homogenization would persist to a large extent 
because, even with some novel options, the recommendations would largely reflect 
the desires of those who are similar to us, but not those of other groups to which we 
might belong in the future and which would be qualitatively different. Given the simi-
larity among members, the digital environment that will be derived from this type of 
systems will necessarily be very homogeneous and immutable, and this would reduce 
the user’s opportunity to discover their potential new interests, preferences, and val-
ues, thus limiting their self-knowledge and life experience. This technique, like the 
previous one, would continue to isolate the user from different lifestyles and deprive 
them of an update of their preferences. The limited knowledge one has of oneself 
regarding what one wants and what one can aspire to limits authenticity because it 
limits the capacity to live according to ideals one would reflectively endorse. Without 
that broad knowledge of other perspectives with which to compare one’s own, it is 
impossible to reflectively endorse the values one holds and, ultimately, to be able to 
claim them as one’s own.

orative filtering technique is one in which groups are automatically and opaquely

created (clustering) and profiles are automatically assigned to them based on a given 
objective. This filtering technique has proven effective in increasing user engage -
ment; however, by prioritizing only that effectiveness, it introduces another twist to 
the problems related to the experience and development of personal identity that we 
have just introduced. The difficulty here lies not only in the fact that the list of char-

necessarily creating groups related to socially recognizable or meaningful categories

of men, fathers, single, aged 43, cat owners, who have watched the film Matrix three 
times, who are iPhone users but do not own a smartwatch, and a long list of other 
characteristics, are statistically inclined to consume a certain product. Perhaps a par-
ticular user could identify with some of these characteristics if they reflected on their 
preferences, beliefs, and desires, but they would hardly identify with all of them, 
and—most importantly—there has been no reflection to select some and not others. 
This highlights how the use of a recommender system based on collaborative filter -
ing contrasts with how individuals make sense of, and reflexively develop, their own 
identity through social categories and the reasons and obligations these encompass 
(Milano et al., 2020; Del Valle & Lara, 2023).
Special mention deserves the criticism that all these types of CR may entail an 
important degradation of certain capacities of great value for autonomy. For some 
authors, this is a problem that extends to most uses of virtual assistants, insofar as 
they involve an outsourcing of important cognitive tasks, which ultimately atrophies 
the human capacities that enable them (Carr, 2014; Lu, 2024). In the case of CRs, 
those tasks would be those closely related to deliberative capacities and willpower. 
This criticism of AI induced deskilling is grounded in long-standing experiments 
demonstrating that the greater the cognitive effort invested in tasks such as memo -
rization, conceptual understanding, or problem-solving, the greater the efficiency in 
performing these operations in the future (Slamecka & Graf, 1978). From this fol -
lows, as a corollary, degradation as the opposite effect: if the effortful use of certain 
cognitive tasks improves functions connected to them, their non-use, through delega-
tion to other agents, will also worsen those functions, as certain experiments have 
demonstrated (van Nimwegen et al., 2006; Burgos et al., 2007; Bastani et al., 2024; 
Kosmyna et al., 2025).
Some authors have attempted to argue that this is not a problem in itself. That it 
ultimately depends on the type of tasks that are delegated. To this end, they introduce 
the distinction between instrumental tasks and those considered intrinsically valu -
able. The main argument is that if the tasks that are delegated are instrumental (and 
they acknowledge that the effect on these can indeed be degenerative), we end up 
with an overal positive final balance, since freeing ourselves from performing certain 
tedious, repetitive, and unimportant tasks can leave us more time and energy to better 
(rationally) devote ourselves to intrinsically valuable tasks (Danaher, 2018; Leyer 
& Schneider, 2021). But to this defense of delegating instrumental tasks, one could 
object that, regardless of the type of task, cognitive outsourcing could lead to the deg-
radation of highly valuable skills, such as those involved in rational choice (search -
ing, comparing, deliberating, exercising self-control, persevering …). In other words,

even making the mere differentiation between instrumental and intrinsically valuable

problems versus the other (W. Lu, 2024, p. 5).
Interrogative Recommenders as Positive Frictions and Their 
Limitations
We have seen the risks to user autonomy posed by recommender systems that operate 
on the basis of data about users’ activity—data about the immediate desires that the 
system captures from a history of choices. An important cause of these problems is 
that RS design prioritizes a smooth user experience that, intentionally or not on the 
part of developers, results in automation biases, deskilling, or difficulties in reflect -
ing on our second-order desires, as we saw above. This fluidity is one of the main 
characteristics sought in the design of user-friendly or ergonomic interfaces. In fact, 
in general, frictions in the human-machine interface—that is, points of difficulty or 
interruption during user interaction with the system—have traditionally been consid -
ered something to avoid in the design of these interfaces. However, recently, certain 
frictions have begun to be treated as potentially positive “behavioral speed bumps,” 
especially in situations that require greater self-control, more reflection, and in con -
texts where automation biases may predominate (Chen & Schmidt, 2024, p. 18). 
Although they had little impact, the first friction proposals consisted of introducing 
delays into the various actions a user could take. Other frictions seek to encourage 
specific actions or alert the user to certain system limitations. Paradigmatic examples 
of the latter include the “read before you post” on X (formerly Twitter), which coun-
teracts the impulsive publication of hoaxes and misinformation, and the now disap -
peared warning in ChatGPT below its input box that encouraged users to verify the 
information provided by the chatbot.
Within the field of RSs, a first positive friction has been to directly ask the user 
about their evaluation criteria. This is what has been attempted with algorithms 
based on a design that feeds on data derived from specific evaluations requested 
from the user. The user must indicate, either numerically or verbally, how much 
they like a particular item. These recommender systems are known as “explicit 
feedback” systems, and here we will call them “interrogative recommenders” 
(IR). This type of RS would represent an advance over those previously dis -
cussed because, in addition to resolving the technical problem of the cold start 
(which occurs when CRs do not have sufficient data on user preferences), they 
could yield better results regarding autonomy. They would escape some of the 
criticisms leveled at CRs insofar as, by asking the user to stop and think about 
their responses to the evaluation request, such responses would be more reflective 
and aligned with the user’s intentions, thereby reducing the aforementioned gap 
between these intentions and immediate desires (Angwald et al., 2021).

However, these techniques also do not serve to give the user what they “want to 
want,” since the user is limited to evaluating only what they already see. If the user 
only sees romantic movies because the system, based on past history, only recom -
mends items of this film genre, when asked to evaluate them, they will only do so for 
such items. Still, they will not have the opportunity to tell the system that they “want 
to want” to watch historical films. For this reason, Krook and Blockx ( 2023) insist 
that, in order for the RS to access those second-order desires that do not appear in 
the user’s history, the user must be given a space and a time within the application to 
think about whether they really want to choose what they are choosing. That is, they 
need to be able to question their first-order desires within the context of the system 
itself.
Other types of IR design aim to advance this objective of allowing some reflec -
tion. For instance, IRs based on questions about topics or categories would require 
greater user effort but would allow them to choose the type of content they want to be 
shown. Certainly, by confronting the user ex ante with the question of what they want 
to choose from, we could be making progress toward eliciting in the user a certain 
second-level preference, since, as in the previous cases, the user would be compelled 
to stop and think—but in this case, not about what is presented to them, but about 
other possible content for choosing.
But Krook and Blockx ( 2023) also see problems with this IR technique. They 
argue that the system could yield biased results, because the question posed to the 
user about their preferred content is framed within pre-established categories that 
may not fully capture all the user’s possible interests. Furthermore, once within those 
categories, we could not prevent the recommendations for individual items from fol-
lowing biased criteria of popularity or efficiency. Popularity bias manifests when the 
algorithm disproportionately promotes items with high historical engagement, effec-
tively silencing the “long tail” of diverse and lesser-known content in favor of what 
is already trending. Simultaneously, efficiency-driven criteria prioritize items that are 
statistically more likely to yield immediate returns, such as clicks or retention, over 
those that might offer deeper personal relevance. This biased tendency of the system 
to recommend certain strategies or items more frequently would then result in greater 
group conformity and filter bubbles, thereby repeating the drawbacks of excessive 
homogenization seen in earlier systems.
Increasing the level of friction, designs have also been proposed that promote 
less unreflective behavior and in which the user acquires more control over the 
personalization process that gives rise to the recommendation. Regarding the 
former, it has been proposed to add mechanisms similar to the “delays” men -
tioned above: an intervention mechanism that forces the user to reflect on and 
become aware of their excessive use of recommendation systems—for example, 
by requiring the user to solve certain cognitive tasks if they want to continue 
using the application, or by allowing them to self-limit the daily time they can 
access the application (Angwald et al., 2021).
On the other hand, the user can be given greater control over how the system 
works, by providing them with the possibility or the requirement, at regular intervals, 
of reconsidering their preferences. Users need to be able to update their preferences 
whenever they wish, rather than only expressing them when they first enter the appli-

cation, or even to reset to default values at any time (Krook & Blockx, 2023; Guo, 
2018; Knijnenburg et al., 2016). Along similar lines, we have proposed elsewhere 
that users be provided with the ability to access, in a comprehensible manner, the 
profile stored by the system and to change characteristics with which they do not 
identify (del Valle & Lara, 2023). Finally, it has been proposed to include in the 
system a customizable interface with various degrees of control, allowing users to 
choose between a Focus mode, intended for when a user has a specific goal, leading 
the interface to remove distractions and recommendation algorithms to facilitate a 
quick “in-and-out” experience, or an Explore mode, designed to broaden the user’s 
horizons by surfacing novel and diverse recommendations that encourage serendipi-
tous discovery (Lukoff et al., 2018).
These proposals for improving IRs may partially address the objections of Krook 
and Blockx ( 2023) regarding the lack of active user involvement and the limited 
personalization of recommendations. The user could be interrupted, through dif -
ferent strategies, in their “blind” following of recommendations. But this raises a 
new problem: when does a friction cease to be positive? Even accepting that these 
cognitive “speed bumps” are positive in the pursuit of second-order desires, would 
the user accept such interruptions? Would they not lose incentives to use applica -
tions that operate with recommenders of this type, thereby depriving themselves of 
the potential benefits these could provide? In general, when frictions are introduced 
into a user–machine interface, the trade-off between the characteristic one wishes to 
enhance (for example, avoiding automation bias, facilitating the exploration of user 
preferences) and excessive paternalism—or, equally counterproductive, an interface 
design so unfriendly that it ends up not being used—must be carefully managed. 
Finally, we cannot ignore the elephant in the room: what incentive would the compa-
nies that develop or offer these recommenders have to include a less fluid interface 
with certain functionality that does not, a priori, seek a direct economic return?
In summary, in this section and the previous one, we have examined the problems 
we consider most important regarding the personal autonomy of RS users. These 
problems are more pronounced in what we have called Conjectural Recommend -
ers. These systems make recommendations based on past behavior (both one’s own 
and that of similar people according to criteria not always known) and moreover 
assume that our desires are fully defined by that behavior—that is, by those choices. 
This type of design promotes the uncritical acceptance of proposed recommenda -
tions, which gives rise to three problems that we have described in detail. The first 
is that interaction with this type of system likely impedes the authentic development 
of preferences due to an excessive homogenization of recommendations. The second 
problem is related to the use of profiles created by the system with the aim of improv-
ing an objective that does not have to be aligned with the user’s typically increasing 
consumption or engagement. We have seen that this entails the impossibility of mak-
ing sense of the proposed recommendations, which can have harmful effects on the 
development of personal identity. The third is the erosion of the deliberative capaci-
ties and willpower that we consider necessary for the exercise of our autonomy.
We have also introduced the concept of friction in the user interface and exempli-
fied some positive frictions in what we have called Interrogative Recommenders. We 
have indicated that, although these frictions (whether IRs on their own or in hybrid

other, more appropriate designs. On the other hand, it must be acknowledged that, 
regardless of whether we are paternalistic or not, we are entering a territory where 
the lack of fluidity may become counterproductive. Can this be achieved without 
demanding so much effort from the user? Can we reach an adequate trade-off in 
which the RS offers an interface that is sufficiently fluid and attractive to users while 
at the same time helping them choose what they truly want to want? In the follow -
ing section, we will present our proposal for a positive friction in the RS domain and 
attempt to respond to these challenges adequately.
Socratic RS
In this section, we aim to present a sketch of recommender system design to help 
overcome the challenges identified in the previous section. A fundamental feature 
of our proposal is already considered in some recent projects: the use of dialogue 
between the system and the user as a key element in the process of generating recom-
mendations. A “Conversational Recommender System” (CoRS) would combine the 
prediction of interests already found in CRs with dialogue through a virtual assistant 
that interacts with users and guides them through the different recommendations, 
allowing them to explore their preferences in a more reflective manner. This dialogue 
would be our proposed positive first friction: the objective is to disrupt the fluidity of 
using the CR and the uncritical selection of elements proposed by the recommender 
system, while simultaneously fostering in the user a conscious reflection on their own 
preferences.
In this recommender design, two techniques are combined. On the one hand, 
the recommendation is based on a knowledge base or collaborative filtering 
found in traditional recommender systems; on the other hand, using these initial 
recommendations as a first output, the capacity of new virtual assistants based 
on Large Language Models (LLM) to provide meaningful dialogue. The basic 
scheme typically involves the user initiating a conversation with a virtual assis -
tant by requesting a recommendation for a specific type of item (e.g., films). The 
system will propose an initial recommendation based on traditional techniques, 
which will not be the final result, as in CRs, but rather the opposite: the start -
ing point. From here, the assistant prompts the user to provide feedback on the 
recommendations, establishing a conversation in which the system captures the 
user’s preferences and thereby enriches the profile. It then proposes recommen -
dations again based on this updated profile, incorporating input from the conver -
sation. On the other hand, the fact that the user participates in the conversation

reflects their interest in a more reflective use of recommendations rather than

applications in specific domains such as book recommendations (Panda & Kaur, 
2023; Lappalainen & Narayanan, 2023), healthcare recommendations (Nastasi et 
al., 2023; Thawkar et al., 2023), or hotel recommendations (Remountakis et al.,

Because they can generate an apparently meaningful and enriching conversation 
for the user, these assistant-recommenders can engage in dialogues that are very simi-
lar to those maintained by humans. They can generate responses that adapt to the 
intentions and nuances of users’ requests, thus facilitating real-time recommenda -
tions that, because they result from dynamic and more engaging interactions, are also 
more personalized and convincing (Vaswani et al., 2017; Brown et al., 2020; Rima 
et al., 2023; Omara et al., 2023; Sohail et al., 2023; Kalirai & Kuzminykh, 2024). 
This dynamic interaction—requesting, when necessary, that issues be clarified and 
that the nuances of the conversation be reflected—leads to a highly immersive user 
experience in which the recommendations are seamlessly integrated into the dia -
logues, improving user satisfaction and engagement (Vaswani et al., 2017; Brown et 
al., 2020; Kalirai & Kuzminykh, 2024; Al-Hasan et al., 2024). The user would end 
up accepting this type of system more, not only because of the interactive process 
itself but also because of the results, insofar as they perceive the recommendations as 
closer to their real-world experiences.
This is because, to achieve its objective, the system is provided with a new input, 
the conversation, which is integrated with other existing data, such as social media 
interactions, Internet of Things devices, location-based services, and more, consider-
ably enriching the user profile managed by the system (Vaswani et al., 2017).
In addition, this design enables users to explore their higher-level preferences, as it 
allows recommendation systems to treat user preferences as dynamic, stratified, and 
subject to negotiation that reconciles conflicting desires or goals, thereby reflecting 
the social aspects that typically intervene in the human recommendation process. 
This is especially useful in scenarios where the user does not know exactly what they 
want, or where their preferences are unstable or complex (Kalirai & Kuzminykh, 
2024). But it is not only useful for that reason. Offering with the dialogue a narrative 
that the user could accept as a reason for their preference would be an essential fac -
tor for improving recommendations. In real life, several factors influence someone’s 
decision. Thus, for example, a user might want to acquire a product out of curiosity 
or due to the influence of others. Or it might be a desire resulting from a firm, well-
considered decision. Therefore, different people may acquire the same product for 
different reasons. One should not, as traditional systems do, treat users equally who, 
while manifesting the same external desire for something, have very different reasons 
for desiring it. CoRSs could represent a significant advance by noting this and seek -
ing to provide users recommedations with which one can best identify rationally and 
in terms of identity.
The possibilities opened by these CoRSs may yield significant advances in effi -
ciency, recommendation updating, and personalization, and may foster user reflection 
on their preferences. However, although this dialogue is necessary for safeguarding 
and enhancing user autonomy, it does not seem sufficient in itself. This is because,

We believe that advancing in this direction of bringing CoRSs closer to autonomy 
can be supported by the ideas proposed by F. Lara and J. Deckers for the design of a

an interlocutor, not providing moral solutions to the user; rather, by questioning the 
user’s statements, it would, like the Socratic midwife, help the user arrive at possible 
answers to their moral questions on their own. From this basic scheme, the fine-tuned 
training of SocrAI with specialized sources and its conversational interface would 
be designed to help the user’s arguments achieve empirical rigor, conceptual clarity, 
argumentative logic, and practical feasibility, enabling the assistant’s constant ques-
tions to serve as a stimulus for the user to reflect on their moral positions and, through 
this, to enter a process of learning and better use of their moral capacities. The choice 
of this SocrAI over its advisory rivals takes a stance within the autonomy trade-off  
that has structured the debate on artificial moral enhancement at least since Klince -
wicz (2016). An advisory system, i.e., an artificial moral reasoning engine presenting

To this end, and drawing inspiration from the aforementioned SocrAI, it would 
be important for the system to be designed to maximise user autonomy in the forma-
tion of their preferences. This requires, above all, that the system should under no 
circumstances provide users with substantive proposals as to what would be a good 
preference. Its functional criteria must be strictly formal, aiming for the improve -
ment of preferences to be, to some degree, the result of a process in which the user 
has reflected on their desires, and has done so according to criteria such as personal 
coherence and integrity, empirical and consequential knowledge of their preferences,

are formal in that they leave untouched who is doing the deliberating, while still 
shaping the conditions under which that deliberation counts as reliable.
It should be acknowledged that describing these criteria as “formal” does not 
amount to claiming that they are normatively inert. Any account of what counts as 
good deliberation, however minimal, carries some commitment about the architec -
ture of rational agency; this is a familiar difficulty for every procedural theory of 
autonomy, not a peculiarity of our proposal (Christman, 2020). The relevant contrast 
is not between a neutral and a value-laden design, but between a design that com -
mits only to the conditions under which a preference can be considered the agent’s 
own—that it be examined, that it be responsive to evidence, that it survive confronta-
tion with alternatives—and one that commits to which preferences the agent should 
end up holding. The Socratic CoRS is substantive in the first, thin sense and remains 
agnostic in the second, thicker sense that is relevant to the charge of paternalism.
This distinction also addresses a natural objection to cognitive pluralism as a design 
criterion: users are routinely exposed to opposing viewpoints in ordinary digital life, 
apparently with little corrective effect, a fact well documented by the literature on 
selective exposure and confirmation bias (Nickerson, 1998; Taber & Lodge, 2006; 
Bakshy et al., 2015). Worden (2019) clarifies why mere exposure falls short: distin -

be paired with a distinct virtue of self-control, understood as the deliberate monitor -
ing of one’s own cognitive biases while engaging with that content, for exposure to 
yield any corrective benefit. Incidental exposure to disagreement, without this further

A similar clarification is needed regarding coherence as a deliberative virtue. We 
do not intend coherence in the strong coherentist sense long criticized in epistemol -
ogy for allowing a belief system—or, here, a preference system—to remain internally 
consistent while disconnected from experience (Sosa, 1980). This is why coherence 
is paired, in our design, with empirical and consequential awareness, which supplies 
the corrective role that pure coherentism lacks on its own.
The point can be put more precisely using the tripartite analysis of competences 
that Sosa develops in virtue epistemology, and that Klincewicz ( 2025) applies to 
preparatory moral technologies: a competence is exercised reliably when the right 
seat—the subject in whom the competence resides—acts in the right situation, draw-
ing on the right internal structure. What distinguishes preparatory technologies, such 
as Stoic robotic nudges or the Socratic assistant, from substitutive ones is that they 
leave the seat of moral or deliberative agency untouched: the user, not the system, 
remains the one who judges. What they legitimately intervene on is situation and 
structure. On this mapping, cognitive pluralism operates on the situation—ensuring 
the user deliberates in confrontation with relevant alternatives rather than in an epis-
temically impoverished environment—while coherence operates on the structure—
ensuring the user’s own reasoning meets a minimal condition of internal consistency 
once that confrontation has taken place. Neither criterion specifies which preference 
the user’s seat should ultimately endorse; both specify conditions under which the 
user’s own exercise of that capacity counts as reliable rather than arbitrary. This is 
precisely the sense in which the Socratic CoRS’s criteria are formal: they constrain 
the reliability of the process without constraining its output.
This choice of a reliabilist rendering of coherence, rather than a responsibilist one, 
is deliberate. On a responsibilist account of intellectual virtue, possessing a virtue 
requires both a competence and a motivation to exercise it well (Zagzebski, 1996; 
Baehr, 2016; Kotsonis, 2023), and, following Baehr (2013), every intellectual virtue 
can further be seen as pursuing an ultimate goal common to all virtues (reliable cog-
nitive contact with one’s own values) through an immediate goal distinctive of each 
virtue—coherence’s immediate goal being internal consistency among one’s reasons, 
cognitive pluralism’s being confrontation with relevant alternatives (see Kotsonis, 
2023, for this distinction applied to an overarching intellectual virtue). A system such

It is now time to consider the extent to which this Socratic CoRS proposal could 
better address the autonomy challenges posed to traditional models, as set out above.
Firstly, it should be made clear that the objective of conversational recommend -
ers, both CoRS and Socratic CoRS, is not to identify the user’s true second-order 
desires—we do not believe that LLMs are equipped for this—but rather to foster 
conscious user reflection on them. This alone would substantially improve the prob-
lems we analyzed in CRs, whose main characteristic is interface fluidity and the 
consequent pursuit of uncritical acceptance of proposed recommendations. We saw 
that this led to excessive homogenization of recommendations and made it difficult 
for the user to make sense of the proposals, which we consider necessary for the 
adequate development of personal identity. With CoRSs, this simply does not occur, 
since the presentation of recommendations is the first step upon which to develop 
the conversation. This would also mitigate, though not fully resolve, the absence 
of social interactions and their role as a self-correction mechanism. 4 The Socratic 
version, moreover, focuses directly on the second-order desires related to those rec -
ommendations and facilitates the user’s reflective endorsement of their own metapre-
ferences, thereby mitigating problems in developing an authentic identity, or at least 
one not surreptitiously influenced.
Regarding the problems introduced by IRs, we saw that these proposals can fall 
into unacceptable paternalism or, equally counterproductive, disincentivize the use of 
these systems. The CoRS could be accused of paternalism if the assistant’s participa-
tion were aimed at directing the user toward certain preferences considered better 
for them. However, in the proposal presented for the Socratic CoRS, if the user is 
directed anywhere, it can only be toward fulfilling the formal requirement that their 
preferences be truly their own and free. This is because what is fostered is a discus -
sion that is as unbiased as possible about one’s own preferences, seeking to center the 
discussion on the user’s second-order desires—that is, on jointly searching for what 
the user wants to want rather than what they want. And all of this, while adhering to 
criteria that are also formal, such as personal coherence and integrity, empirical and 
consequential knowledge of one’s preferences, and choosing from cognitive plural -
ism, which can hardly be considered a condemnable paternalism.

Conclusion
This article has examined the relationship between recommender systems and human 
autonomy, arguing that the dominant design paradigms in current RSs pose signifi -
cant threats to the authentic formation and development of user preferences. We have 
shown that these threats operate at multiple levels: through the narrowing of the 
preference landscape, the erosion of deliberative capacities, and the impediment to 
personal identity development.
This is particularly true of the systems we call Conjectural Recommenders (CRs), 
which rely on implicit behavioral data to generate predictions, systematically under-
mining autonomy by conflating users’ observed choices with their genuine prefer -
ences. By ignoring the gap between first-order desires and the deeper second-order 
desires that constitute authentic autonomy in the Frankfurtian and Dworkinian sense, 
CRs trap users in homogeneous digital environments that reinforce past behavior, 
inhibit identity development, and progressively degrade the very deliberative capaci-
ties required for human autonomy.
We have introduced the concept of positive friction, which we have applied to 
what we have called Interrogative Recommenders (IRs)—systems that incorporate 
explicit user feedback. While these represent an advance by prompting users to artic-
ulate their evaluations, we argued that they remain insufficient for accessing second-
order desires. We have also introduced a trade-off in the design of positive frictions in 
this domain: the more friction we introduce, the greater the risk of either paternalism 
or user disengagement. This balance constitutes a central design challenge for any RS 
that aspires to protect autonomy.
Our principal contribution has been to propose the Socratic CoRS as a two-
layered friction design that addresses the limitations identified in both CRs and 
IRs. The first friction, the conversational element, disrupts uncritical acceptance 
of recommendations by turning them into starting points for dialogue. The second 
friction, the Socratic orientation, goes further by directing that dialogue toward 
the user’s metapreferences, employing only formal criteria (coherence, empiri -
cal knowledge, cognitive pluralism) rather than substantive conceptions of the 
good. The goal is not for the system to discover the user’s “true” second-order 
desires—an epistemic task we do not attribute to LLMs—but to foster the user’s 
own reflective engagement with their metapreferences. This design avoids the 
charge of paternalism precisely because it does not prescribe what users should 
prefer, but rather fosters the conditions under which preferences can be authenti -
cally formed. Regarding the disincentive problem, we argue that the persuasive 
power of reason-backed preferences, the sense of ownership over deliberatively

Several limitations of this proposal should be acknowledged. First, as we have 
conceded, the system does not resolve the problem identified by Lu ( 2024) regard-
ing the irreplaceable role of genuine social interactions as a corrective mechanism 
for autonomous decision-making. Second, the tension between autonomy-enhancing 
friction and user acceptance remains unresolved. Market incentives for companies to 
adopt such designs are uncertain, especially in environments driven by attention and 
engagement metrics. Finally, the reliance on LLMs—which, as “stochastic parrots,” 
lack genuine rational capacities—means that the quality of the Socratic dialogue will 
always depend on the sophistication of the fine-tuning and the user’s own willingness 
to engage.
Despite these open challenges, we contend that the transition toward an auton -
omy-sensitive framework for recommender systems is both a normative neces -
sity and a practical imperative. As algorithmic mediation increasingly governs 
the informational landscape of our daily lives, the central question is no longer 
whether these architectures influence human agency, but rather how they ought to 
be designed to safeguard it. The Socratic Conversational Recommender represents 
a substantial advancement in embedding deliberative agency within the very logic 
of recommendation. By transcending traditional models that merely extrapolate 
future behavior from past data, the Socratic approach ensures that technology func -
tions not as a catalyst for impulsive consumption, but as a medium for self-reflec -
tion. Ultimately, such systems empower users to exercise true digital autonomy, 
enabling them to choose not merely on the basis of immediate, first-order prefer -
ences, but through an alignment with their metapreferences, effectively bridging 
the gap between what we happen to desire and what we truly want to want.
