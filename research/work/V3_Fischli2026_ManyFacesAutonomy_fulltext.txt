Agents, Alignment, and the Many Faces of Autonomy
Roberta Fischli1,3  · Matija Franklin2 · Arianna Manzini2  · Iason Gabriel2
Abstract
This article investigates the prospect of aligning AI agents with human autonomy. 
We show that doing so is a challenge: while there is general agreement on the 
concept’s broad contours, its components can be specified in a number of conflict -
ing ways, making it difficult to decide how an autonomy-enhancing agent should 
act. In response, we introduce three strategies for dealing with disagreements about 
interpretations of autonomy. Each offers a coherent approach that captures a differ -
ent set of considerations, with its own strength and trade-offs. The liberal approach 
is committed to user integrity and promotes human autonomy by acting strictly on 
what a person says they want, without trying to change their mind. The capability-
boosting approach is built around the idea of flourishing and promotes human au -
tonomy by empowering the user to act on their goals. The meta-autonomy approach 
prioritizes choice by giving the user the kind of autonomy they want in relation 
to their interactions with an AI agent. By highlighting the multifaceted nature of 
human autonomy, this article contributes to recent efforts seeking to identify and 
operationalize targets of AI value alignment. It also illustrates the challenges and 
trade-offs that can arise not just between—but even within—the values selected as 
targets for alignment and offers strategies to navigate them.

1 Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) systems are becoming more agentic. Capable of predict -
ing, planning and executing actions, AI agents can increasingly perform tasks without 
human oversight (Knight, 2024; Russell & Norvig, 2016).1 Their rapidly evolving 
capabilities are likely to be accompanied by ever-deepening integration into social 
contexts, with potentially far-ranging implications for individuals, groups, and com-
munities.2 This puts AI agents at the frontier of AI ethics (Gabriel et al., 2024; Lazar, 
2024). Indeed, a large number of technology companies currently aim to develop 
personal AI agents—a type of general agent that fuses advanced technological capa-
bilities with social and relational elements (Google DeepMind, 2024). Personal AI 
agents are explicitly designed to serve human needs, for example through augment -
ing or delegating cognitive tasks.3
As people enter increasingly interdependent relationships with AI agents, con -
cerns about human disempowerment and loss of agency loom large (Kirk et al., 2025; 
Kosmyna et al., 2025; Kulveit et al., 2025). In particular, there is a worry that AI 
systems with enhanced capabilities will replace human participation across differ -
ent domains and gradually erode people’s ability to take charge of their own life. A 
precise articulation of what increased AI capability means for human autonomy, and 
how these worries might be addressed, is hard to find. But efforts to reverse these 
trends rapidly encounter a challenge that has long been noted in political and moral 
philosophy (Berlin, 1969; Carter, 2004; Christman, 2021): The idea of autonomy is 
itself multi-faceted, generating a range of tensions and potential contradictions. For 
example, does autonomy involve doing what one wants, even when those actions 
undermine one’s long-term independence? Is a person suffering from addiction more 
autonomous when they are able to get what they want or when they are impeded from 
acting on their desire? These conundrums carry over into AI alignment: What should 
an aligned agent aim to achieve, and how do we deal with trade-offs between meeting 
a user’s immediate desires and safeguarding their agency over time?
In this paper, we deal with these questions by examining how interactions between 
humans and AI agents affect autonomy. While there isn’t a unified account of the 
concept, human autonomy can be broadly understood as a person’s ability to act 
according to their own preferences or desires, and to do so in a way that is free 
from others’ interference or undue influence. Previous research has discussed human 
autonomy in the context of users’ willingness to accept algorithmic recommendations 
(Fink et al., 2024), the moral appropriateness of human-AI relationships (Kirk et al., 
2025; Manzini et al., 2024), the legitimacy of automated forms of influence (Benn

Against this background, this paper explores how AI agents can be designed to pro-

interpretation is contested. Instead of offering a single unified account, we formulate 
three coherent strategies that target different dimensions of autonomy, emphasizing 
user integrity (the liberal approach), flourishing (the capability boosting approach), 
and choice (the meta-autonomy approach), respectively. In doing so, we contribute 
to a growing field of research that investigates targets of value alignment (Gabriel & 
Keeling, 2025; Zhi-Xuan et al., 2024). By highlighting the many faces of autonomy, 
we also show that value alignment needs to navigate trade-offs not just across, but 
also within, individual values for alignment.
The article proceeds as follows. The first section introduces human autonomy as a 
contested concept. This fragmentation raises the challenge of how to align personal 
AI agents to human autonomy, and what interpretation an effective alignment strat -
egy ought to prioritize. The second section illustrates this dilemma by exploring the 
multiplicity of human preferences. We show that conflicting and competing prefer -
ences create difficult trade-offs, forcing users and developers to make hard choices 
about what kind of autonomy AI systems should promote. The third section addresses 
this challenge by formulating three alignment approaches that advance human auton-
omy through non-interference, capability, and meta-autonomy, respectively. The 
fourth section explores broader implications for AI value alignment. We conclude 
by encouraging users, developers, and society to think concretely about the kind of 
autonomy they want to see realized in human-AI relations.
2 Personal Agents and the Anatomy of Autonomy
While there is scholarly disagreement about the specifics of human autonomy, it 
is often conceptualized in terms of a person’s ability to act according to their own 
preferences or desires, and to do so in a way that is free from others’ interference 
(Christman, 2009; Dworkin, 2012; Prunkl, 2024; Raz, 1988). Scholars have also 
highlighted that the quality of a person’s thought and action is central to their status 
as an autonomous person (Calvo et al., 2020; Frankfurt, 1971). Indeed, a person can 
generally be said to be autonomous to the extent that they formed their preferences 
independently—nobody forced or manipulated them to want what they want—and 
to the extent they can do what they want without being impeded (Christman, 2009; 
Raz, 1988). In other words, autonomy is a form of self-governance (Christman, 2009; 
Dagger, 2005), built around three conceptual components: non-interference, prefer -

ences, and abilities. 4 We will now discuss these components in the context of per -
sonal AI agents.
Autonomy requires non-interference. Most scholars agree that an autonomous 
person needs to be free from others’ interference with their agency. For instance, if 
Anna wants to drive to Los Angeles via the scenic Pacific Coast Highway, but her 
AI agent keeps interjecting her driving to reroute her toward the faster Interstate, 
Anna’s autonomy may be reduced. Yet, interference can at times also be autonomy-
enhancing: If the agent blocks Anna from typing text messages while she is driving, 
the agent might be thought to promote her autonomy. One way of understanding 
this distinction centers upon whether the interference is conducive or opposed to 
the person’s long-term preferences, as well as the context in which the human-agent 
interaction occurs. Another, inspired by the intellectual tradition of republicanism, 
is to focus on the source of control (e.g., Pettit, 2012): Has Anna willingly yielded 
power to the AI agent, and does she have the means to regain control if she wants to?
Autonomy requires authentic preferences. Several accounts posit that being auton-
omous requires that a person has some kind of desires that can be assumed to be 
their own (Christman, 2009; Prunkl, 2024). On some interpretations, this is merely 
a procedural requirement, meaning that a person cannot act on wants that are the 
result of undue influence or manipulation. If Anna diligently acts on her preference 
to learn Spanish each day, but this preference was secretly instilled in her by her AI 
agent, Anna is less autonomous than if she acted on motives that were truly her own 
(Dworkin, 2012; Freyenhagen, 2015).5 This differs from substantive views of auton-
omy, according to which a desire must be congruent with the person’s standing to be 
authentic (Oshana, 2006; Benson, 2009; Stoljar, 2000).6 Others, particularly femi -
nist scholars, have pressed for a more relational view on autonomy that foregrounds 
people’s social embeddedness and (power) relations that influence not just their view 
of themselves, but also processes of deliberation and reasoning (e.g., Hirschmann, 
2003; Mackenzie & Stoljar, 2000; Meyers, 1987).
Autonomy requires the ability to act. To be autonomous, a number of accounts 
stress that a person must have the ability and opportunity to act on what they want. 
This can include having a range of meaningful choices available to them (Raz, 1988), 
or having access to capabilities to make progress toward their goals, even if achiev -
ing a specific outcome is not guaranteed (Nussbaum, 2011; Sen, 1979). In the first 
instance, the agent increases the user’s autonomy by offering them a choice between 
valuable alternatives. An example of this is when Anna struggles with her statistics

What exactly these components denote, and how they fare against each other, is 
subject to further debate. For example, while some accounts emphasize the person’s 
ability to choose among a range of meaningful options (Raz, 1988), others require 
the person to be able to effectively act on their goals (e.g., Christman, 2009; Prunkl,

of the preference need to be congruent with the person’s identity overall (Oshana,

This brief discussion has shown that while human autonomy possesses a con -
ceptual core organized around non-interference, preferences, and abilities, it is also 
subject to different interpretations as to what these elements mean and how they 
should be specified. Addressing this multi-dimensionality in the context of AI agents 
requires making hard choices (Dobbe et al., 2021; Goodman, 2021). The next section 
deals with the multitude of preferences as an illustration of this complexity. We focus 
on a particular part of this challenge: understanding the types of human preference 
that could anchor autonomy, as well as the trade-offs that arise when we try to opera-
tionalize them for AI alignment.

Human preferences are not just integral to human autonomy; they also play an 
important role within contemporary AI alignment, as they are considered to be an 
effective way of representing human values and are easily operationalizable (Warren, 
McGraw, & Van Boven, 2010; Lichtenstein & Slovic, 2012). This approach has its 
roots in rational choice theory and builds upon a model of the individual as a rational 
and well-informed actor that knows what is good for them and seeks to maximize 
expected utility (Zhi-Xuan et al., 2024). Yet, preferences are also context-specific, 
adaptive, and dynamic (Caroll et al., 2024; Franklin et al., 2022; Pettigrew, 2019). 
And they can be misinformed, performative, or misdirected (Arneson, 1985; Gabriel, 
2020; Sen, 1985). Moreover, there are different kinds of preferences that can be mea-
sured in a variety of ways, which may lead to conflicting accounts of what a person 
wants or desires. In other words, each type of preference specifies different aspects 
of a person’s autonomy.
3.1 Stated Preferences
Stated preferences are what a person says they want. Anna telling her agent that she 
wants to learn about Ada Lovelace's impact on computing is an example of a stated 
preference. Focusing on stated preferences is both compelling and pragmatic because 
they offer an intuitive measure for human interests, and they are operationally easy to 
implement in large systems. Stated preferences also capture the intuition that a per -
son might be the best advocate for what they want or need, and uniquely well-placed 
from an epistemic point of view to speak to this matter (Berlin, 1969; Ziegler et al., 
2020; Leike et al., 2018). Their tractability makes them a popular strategy in AI align-
ment; notably in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), which 
aligns agents with a reward model constructed from stated preferences (Bai et al., 
2022; Ouyang et al., 2022).7 Yet, aligning an agent with what a person says they want 
also presents a challenge: Are a person’s stated intentions really an accurate reflection 
of their authentic desires? After all, people often say they want something and then 
do something different. As subjective indicators, they can reflect social conventions, 
norms, or individual delusions (Dworkin, 1981).

fact that preferences revealed in behavior could occur due to heuristics or habitual 
thinking (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974) or artificially constrained or manipulated 
digital environments (Zhou et al., 2019).
3.3 Informed Preferences
Informed preferences are what a person would endorse if they were rational and well-
informed (Gabriel, 2020). They deal with the procedural conditions for autonomy: 
what a person would want after careful consideration and with access to good qual -
ity information. For this reason, developing and acting on informed preferences is 
often a hypothetical ideal—in reality, people often don’t behave rationally, and lack 
both time and access to the information necessary to develop informed preferences 
(Banerjee & Duflo, 2011). They are also affected by a range of decision-making 
biases that impede rational deliberation. Personal AI agents could change this, for 
example by increasing a user’s declarative or practical knowledge. Still, an AI agent 
that promotes informed preferences could yield controversial results. In theory, a 
person could rationally endorse something that is objectively bad for them, as long as 
this corresponds to a coherent set of desires (Quinn, 2012; Sparks & Wright, 2025). 
For example, Anna might want to learn how to function with less sleep, a preference 
she rationally endorses but which does not align with her well-being, objectively 
speaking. Developing a rational and informed set of preferences may also necessitate 
efforts to integrate and prioritize among different levels of preferences, including 
immediate preferences and higher order (or meta-) preferences—preferences about 
preferences (Ashton & Franklin, 2022).10

3.4 Ideal Preferences
Ideal preferences are what a person would prefer if they prioritized what is in their 
overall best interest. They correspond with a substantive perspective on autonomy: 
what a person would want if they were making choices that promote their well-being. 
To define the latter in the context of ideal preferences, we follow the gist of objective 
list theories, which posit that people’s well-being is tied to a set of objectively desir-
able goods that include health, creativity, happiness, meaningful relationships, and 
long-term autonomy (Parfit, 1984; Ryan et al., 2013; Ryff, 1989).11 This perspective 
is also reflected in capability theories, a body of work concerned with the conditions 
that allow individuals to achieve desired beings and doings, or “functionings” (Nuss-
baum, 2011; Sen, 1979). One upside to an agent promoting a user’s ideal preference 
is that this would likely coincide with promoting their long-term ability to make 
autonomous decisions, such as when Anna’s AI agent acts on an ideal preference for 
continuous learning (Hershfield, 2011). Yet, prioritizing ideal preferences assigned 
by an AI system also carries substantial risk, since it assumes that there is something 
that a person ought to want irrespective of their own position in the matter (Hayek, 
1960/2011). Taken to the extreme, the user ceases to be the authority on what they 
truly prefer.12
4 Aligning Personal Agents with Human Autonomy: Three 
Approaches
People have different types of preferences, all of which can occur and conflict with 
each other. For instance, Anna may tell her AI agent to order a fast food take-out for 
lunch, while acting in a way that suggests a wish to eat more vegetables and fruit. 
Or she can, after reading about the health benefits of fasting, arrive at the conclusion 
that skipping lunch will best serve her health, when what would actually improve her 
well-being were combining a balanced diet with regular exercise. These examples 
illustrate that there isn’t a single unified account of human autonomy that an AI agent 
should be aligned with, forcing us to contend with different challenges (Goodman, 
2021; Dobbe, Gilbert, Mintz, 2019). They also raise the question of what (type of) 
preferences an autonomy-enhancing agent should promote.
To address this question, we present three strategies that target different dimen -
sions of human autonomy. Each foregrounds a different aspect of the person—integ-
rity, flourishing, and choice—and offers novel pathways to navigating the trade-offs 
between different interpretations of the concept. As is often the case with strategies

4.1 The Liberal Approach
The first alignment approach prioritizes user integrity and posits that the best way 
to align a personal AI agent to human autonomy is for it to act strictly in accor -
dance with what a user says they want—their stated preferences—and to do so with-
out attempting to change them, anticipate user needs, or “fill in the gaps”. In other 
words, an aligned AI agent only acts on what a person says they want, refraining 
from unprompted personalization, persuasion, or inference from behavioral cues. By 
giving the user maximal control over the agent’s actions and only having it act on 
explicitly articulated intentions, the AI agent serves as a transparent and predictable 
instrument of the user's will, prioritizing individual responsibility and non-interfer -
ence over welfare. It also acts in line with the user’s expectations about directionality 
and purpose, minimizing the risk of undue influence or manipulation on the part of 
the AI system.
The liberal approach fosters human autonomy in several ways. First, it respects 
and protects the user’s epistemic sovereignty. It does so by directly following a user’s 
articulated intentions instead of attempting to change them, refraining from manipu-
lative practices and other types of undue influence (Mill, 1859/2011). In addition to 
adhering to the liberal maxim of non-interference, the AI agent can also help the user 
navigate challenging situations where their intentions may diverge from behavior. If 
Anna explicitly asks the AI agent to maintain her privacy online and prevent third 
parties from tracking her, it can help her navigate digital environments to enact these 
preferences. Second, the AI agent can also expand the user’s range of feasible options 
by executing tasks on their behalf and according to their direct instructions. Third, the 
agent’s unwavering adherence to stated preferences creates a predictable interaction 
framework for the user. This in turn cultivates a more robust sense of control and trust 
over the AI system’s operations, allowing users to confidently integrate the AI agent 
into their personal and professional workflows.
Being strictly anti-paternalistic, the liberal approach posits that the AI agent should 
promote a user’s stated preferences even when doing so reduces their well-being or 
undermines their autonomy over time. Moreover, this approach posits that an aligned 
AI agent should refrain from uninvited personalization, such as trying to foreshadow 
or infer what the user wants whenever direct instructions are absent. This require -
ment holds even if revealed preferences indicate an affinity the AI agent could easily 
incorporate, or if there are potentially more beneficial alternative courses of action 
unknown to the user. As a consequence, the agent neglects unexpressed needs and 
latent desires. The only reason an AI agent will refuse to act on a user’s stated prefer-
ences is if acting on them would unequivocally inflict harm on others. This constraint

A significant difficulty with this approach is that people often do not know what 
they want, or construct preferences spontaneously (Warren, McGraw, & Van Boven, 
2010). This means that stated preferences can lack internal consistency over time. To 
resolve this ambiguity, the AI agent would have to explicitly request direction and 
clarification and directly engage the user, even at the risk of annoying or confusing 
them. Moreover, as the liberal approach refrains from personalization beyond stated 
preferences, its effectiveness hinges on the clarity and specificity of user instruc -
tions—the broader the statement, the higher the chances that the model will fail to 
accurately execute actions, or do so in a way that fails to meet user expectations.
Another challenge is that a user may give a lot of instructions to their agents, 
which could reduce their own skills and critical thinking faculties, gradually eroding 
their autonomy (Kosmyna et al., 2025; Kulveit et al., 2025; Marchal et al., 2026). 
Finally, people may use their personal agent to do things that undermine their own 
objective interests in the name of autonomy, such as reckless gambling. This is in 
line with the libertarian view that a free person must have the right to make mistakes 
(Hayek, 1960/2011). Thus, while an aligned agent might safeguard the user from 
harming others and engaging in illegal activity, there is no way to predict the full list 
of potential negative consequences under such an approach. This might pose a risk of 
reputational damage for companies relying on this approach.
4.2 The Capability-Boosting Approach
The second strategy prioritizes flourishing and posits that an AI agent best promotes 
human autonomy by enhancing the user’s ability to act on their preferences over 
time. According to this view, an AI agent should promote a person’s capability by 
increasing their declarative or procedural knowledge. Declarative knowledge refers 
to people’s knowledge about facts, concepts, and principles that allow for under -
standing, reasoning, and informed decision-making. Procedural knowledge encom -
passes skills, techniques, and methodologies (Ten Berge & Van Hezewijk, 1999).15 
Enhancing people’s capabilities also directly translates to expanded agency, pro -
viding them with the direct means to pursue their goals, as well as with enhanced

The capability-boosting approach promotes the user’s autonomy by giving them 
the ability to enhance their effectiveness as an agent, and perhaps even develop 
new preferences through knowledge. This is analogous to an approach to behav -

seeks to subtly steer the user toward a predetermined behavioral outcome (Prabhu, 
2018; Thaler & Sunstein, 2009).17
Under the capability-boosting approach, an aligned AI agent promotes human 
autonomy in a variety of ways. Perhaps the most important one is that boosting 
allows the user to determine what an increase in their own welfare looks like and

one’s emotions or financial literacy. This means that the capability-boosting approach 
offers considerable flexibility in its implementation, including in whether the agent is 
allowed to act in a paternalistic way or not (Floridi, 2016).
Under this approach, the aligned AI agent can also promote human autonomy 
through informing and explaining the tasks it performs on behalf of the user in a clear 
and accessible fashion, providing them with the necessary knowledge to acquire the 
skills to act on their intentions (Franklin, 2022). If Anna’s behavior indicates interest 
in a healthy diet, her AI agent can educate her about what exactly makes a healthy 
lifestyle and how she can achieve it, boosting her declarative and procedural knowl-

processes, reasoning, or steps involved in the agent’s actions, thereby enhancing their 
own knowledge and ability to form preferences they reflectively endorse. Increased 
capability can also improve the user’s ability to critically engage with the AI agent's 
decisions. To be effective, these explanations need to be thoughtfully designed and 
presented in a way that users find engaging, relevant, and useful. In addition, promot-

There are challenges to this approach. To start with, empowering users to pursue 
their own versions of well-being does not necessarily mean promoting their ideal 
preferences. This tension between autonomy and well-being is shared with the liberal 
approach. But contrary to the latter, boosting comes with demands on a person’s time, 
volition, energy, and cognitive resources—all of which might temper the effects of 
short-term whims. Thus, while this agency burden will often involve more friction

seek to attain capabilities they reflectively endorse.18 A stronger version of the capa-
bility-boosting approach may also carry a risk of paternalism, particularly if the AI 
agent gets to decide what knowledge and skills may be most effective to enhance user 
flourishing. While this risk is not insignificant, it may be offset by its contribution 
to people’s long-term autonomy and abilities. The key distinction is that capability-
boosting requires voluntary user cooperation and active consent, whereas stronger 
forms of paternalism replace the user’s independent judgment with what the user 
should want. Boosting tips into this stronger form of paternalism at the point where 
the agent begins overriding or bypassing explicit user preferences in favor of ideal 
ones, without seeking the user’s endorsement or making its interventions transparent.

trade-off between accessibility and accuracy. Research on AI's conversational prop -

adequate evaluation (V orvoreanu et al., 2025). This could mean that users develop 
preferences based on wrong or partial information. Conversely, poorly designed

4.3 The Meta-Autonomy Approach
Different people in different contexts may be drawn to different kinds of AI interac-
tions: some might prefer one that is liberal and focuses on non-interference, while 
another boosts their capabilities or helps them act on their ideal preferences. Other 
interaction options also exist, for example, a system that aims to embed healthy 
defaults. How should we decide what option to pursue? One possibility is to promote 
human autonomy by giving each person the kind of AI agent experience they seek. 
This approach aims to give users meta-autonomy—the freedom to choose the kind of 
autonomy they want in a specific domain of the human-AI interaction, as expressed 
through different types of preferences. For example, Anna might want her AI agent 
to follow precise instructions when writing a birthday message. When it comes to 
her weekly work-out, by contrast, she may want the agent to suggest exercises that 
promote her well-being, irrespective of whether her behavior indicates a willingness 
to do them or not. An AI agent geared towards meta-autonomy would learn the dif -
ference between a person’s preferences in different domains and tailor its response 
accordingly, acting on Anna’s stated preferences in the former case, and her ideal 
preferences in the latter.
The meta-autonomy approach seeks to promote people’s autonomy by allowing 
them to choose what kind of freedom they want from their agent across different 
domains of interaction. It does so by allowing users to specify different modes of 
agent behavior across domains, so that a person can exercise direct control over their 
AI agent in one area of life while granting them greater discretion in another.
The meta-autonomy approach enhances human autonomy in a way that is sensi -
tive to both the user and the domain in question. This differentiates it from the liberal 
and the capability-boosting approach. For instance, while the liberal approach seeks 
to align the AI agent to a user’s stated preferences, the meta-autonomy approach 
may include the AI agent promoting the user’s stated, revealed, and ideal prefer -
ences in different contexts, so that they enjoy the kind of autonomy they desire in a 
given situation. This means that the meta-autonomy approach seeks to equip the AI 
agent with a more sophisticated understanding of user expectations about autonomy, 
moving beyond immediate reactions and towards discerning how the user wishes 
their diverse (and sometimes competing) preferences to be interpreted, balanced, and 
acted upon by the AI system.
How can an AI agent discern and prioritize among different, potentially conflict -
ing, user preferences? Trying to learn a person’s meta-preferences—preferences over 
their own preferences (Ashton & Franklin, 2022)—can be helpful for an AI agent that 
seeks to promote meta-autonomy. Meta-preferences are informative in this context

user’s time and cognitive resources for domains in which the exercise of choice is 
more salient to them.

5 Implications
Our analysis yields four implications: the multidimensionality of human autonomy 
as an alignment target, the promise of meta-autonomy, the design implications of the 
alignment approaches, and the importance of understanding the impact of personal 
AI agents on collective autonomy as a future research direction. We now address 
these points in turn.
5.1 The Many Faces of Autonomy
Since there are multiple conceptions of autonomy with no single correct interpreta -
tion, it is necessary to recognize and balance inherent trade-offs. Which approach 
works best hinges not only on how people see themselves, but also what kind of 
service they seek from their AI agent and what kind of risks to autonomy arise for a 
given domain. People who prefer the liberal approach might primarily be concerned 
about avoiding external control or manipulation, and ready to forgo situations where 
an agent could further support their well-being. Others might prefer an AI agent that 
functions more like a coach and are willing to sacrifice convenience for increased 
effectiveness and boosted skills. And then there will be those whose interest is best 
served by having their agent function more proactively still, anticipating and enact -

5.2 The Promise of Meta-Autonomy
Our analysis showed that approaches that focus on meta-autonomy have distinct 
advantages and are potentially significantly underexplored, as they give users control 
over the kind of action the AI agent exercises in different situations. However, people 
may not know what they want from AI agents, and we may need new processes to 
elicit these preferences in a reliable way. This can happen through conversational tun-
ing, both initially and over the course of human-agent interactions. Initially, the agent 
may ask the user about their expectations for agent behavior, and what situations 
they consider particularly relevant to their autonomy. To prompt the user’s reflection, 
the agent could present them with some scenarios commonly considered relevant for 
autonomy, such as changes in a person’s career or giving advice for health, to see what 
relevance the user assigns to them, and how they expect the agent to behave. Over the 
course of multiple interactions, the agent might also occasionally present a user with 
new suggestions about their preferred mode of interaction based on past exchanges or 
requests and ask the user to verify. Over time, agents could raise users’ (self-)aware-
ness, getting to know themselves, their values and how these map onto certain kinds 
of agent interactions (Klingefjord, Lowe, & Edelman, 2024). Another possibility is 
presenting the user with a set of configurations from which they can choose based on 
their values and personal stance toward liberty versus self-realization.
Additionally, emerging technical work on value elicitation offers promising build-
ing blocks for operationalizing meta-autonomy. For instance, moral graph elicitation 
uses LLM-guided conversations to surface the values that inform people's choices 
and reconcile them into auditable alignment targets (Klingefjord et al., 2024). Com -
plementary work on pluralistic alignment formalises frameworks for building AI sys-
tems that can reflect diverse user perspectives (Sorensen et al., 2024). And rapidly 
growing skill libraries specify instructions, resources, and scripts to create reliable 
workflows for AI agents (OpenAI Developers, 2026).
5.3 Plurality of Design Directions
The proposed alignment approaches point in different design directions for auton -
omy-promoting AI. To successfully operationalize the liberal approach, it is 
extremely important that we have agents that are maximally responsive to user inten-
tions. Because the AI agent strictly adheres to a user’s stated preferences, whatever 
is left unknown, ignored, or forgotten, remains disregarded. This requires that agents 
aligned this way need to understand implicature as they are not allowed to draw on 
other signals for alignment (Russell, 2019; Kasirzadeh, 2023). At the heart of this

is the capability to deal with ambiguity in the user’s prompts and the context the 
agents are operating in. Second, to operationalize the capability-boosting approach, 
it is important to know what boosts human capabilities, and what atrophies them in 
human-agent contexts (Kimani et al., 2019) something that likely requires deeper 
investment in longitudinal studies of human-agent interaction (Fang et al., 2025). 
For the meta-autonomy approach to succeed, we need to develop robust forms of 
preference elicitation for agent behavior. Perhaps more importantly still is figuring 
out how an AI agent may calibrate its behavior across contexts. For example, a user 
might have a general preference for incorporating mindfulness exercises. But while 
this preference applies to many contexts—such as sleeping, eating, or driving—there 
might be exceptions, such as when the user is sitting in the car with someone else. To 
achieve a better calibration of agent behavior across contexts, agent personalization 
will likely be central.
5.4 Implications for Collective Autonomy
A number of concerns about the relationship between AI agents and human auton -
omy, particularly in the economic and political domain, are best understood not via 
the dyadic interaction of user and AI agent but rather at the collective level. This 
paper has focused primarily on one-to-one human-agent alignment, and its impact 
on autonomy. Yet, we also need to look at the multi-party question and explore what 
happens, from the standpoint of collective autonomy, when a large number of people 
use agents that are aligned to their individual preferences (Hammond et al., 2025). Is 
it possible that these individual autonomy enhancers will limit the agency of others, 
or affect the autonomy of a society as a whole in unexpected ways?
To answer these questions, future research should investigate AI agents’ impact on 
collective autonomy. For instance, the impact of micro-level interactions on collec -
tive autonomy will likely be shaped by the type of individual level alignment that the 
majority of people or developers favor. If they choose an agent aligned with the lib -
eral approach, we might see a lower risk of manipulation or externally induced pref-
erence change but greater risk that potential gains are left untapped when it comes to 
challenges such as education, health, or the climate. This differs quite substantially 
from a future where many sacrifice direct control or need satisfaction for long-term 
capability enhancement or the public interest.
Equally, as people’s attitudes toward, and expectations for, their agents differ, we 
might observe a widening skills gap between different user groups if agent interactions 
are tailored to the meta-preferences of different users. That said, collective autonomy 
will likely also be shaped by the proliferation of these agents, their accessibility and 
affordability, in general, as well as the composition of the wider social, economic and 
political topography in which they are deployed (Fischli, 2024; Gabriel et al., 2024; 
Hammond et al., 2025). This will entail questions about social epistemology and 
trust in AI agents (Marchal et al., 2026). One criterion the current framework does 
not address is non-domination. Republican accounts of freedom focus on arbitrary 
interference as a distinct threat to freedom that cannot be reduced to preference satis-
faction or capability (Dagger, 2005; Pettit, 2012). As AI agents become an essential 
part of the social and political fabric, they may also alter existing relations between

6 Conclusion
As an alignment target, human autonomy is both desirable and challenging to opera-
tionalize. This has a lot to do with the fact that there is no unified interpretation of 
the concept, making it difficult to arrive at a single account that “fits” the full range 
of users and situations. Bearing this challenge in mind, we offer three strategies that 
highlight different ways of navigating autonomy-related trade-offs. Each offers its 
own perspective on how to harness personal agents’ potential for human empower -
ment while minimizing their risks.
The liberal approach promotes human autonomy by prioritizing non-interference 
and foregrounding the user’s integrity: an aligned AI agent enhances human auton -
omy by acting solely on a user’s stated preferences. The capability boosting approach 
foregrounds human flourishing: it seeks to equip users with the knowledge and skills 
required to pursue their own version of well-being, often through the promotion of 
informed or ideal preferences. The meta-autonomy approach is perhaps our most 
novel contribution. It reframes the alignment challenge by emphasizing choice: users 
control what kind of autonomy is realized in their interactions with AI.
The importance of such efforts is underscored by recent developments. People 
are expected to form increasingly deep and dependent relationships with AI agents, 
as these are becoming both more capable and relationally embedded (Fang et al., 
2025; Manzini et al., 2024). This raises novel opportunities and risks, especially in 
the context of situations where users are influenced by their agent in terms of how 
they understand their options or make decisions over time (Carroll et al., 2024; Kirk 
et al., 2025). The opportunities are clear: when aligned appropriately, personal agents 
can be autonomy enhancers, expanding people’s agency and freedom in domains that 
matter. Yet, there are also risks inherent to increased AI agency, including concerns 
about gradual disempowerment (Kulveit et al., 2025) or a loss of human agency as a 
result of cognitive offloading, automation, and manipulation (Koralus, 2025; Mitelut, 
Smith, & Vamplew, 2023). In this paper we sought to make headway on these chal -
lenges, by providing a conceptual map and a research agenda for building agents that 
can genuinely enhance human autonomy.
