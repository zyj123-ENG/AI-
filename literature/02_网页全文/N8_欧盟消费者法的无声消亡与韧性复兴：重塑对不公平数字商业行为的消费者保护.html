Namysłowska1  9859 Accesses 18 Citations Explore all metrics Abstract This article examines the silent death of EU consumer law in the face of digital transformation. It argues that the traditional consumer protection framework—once grounded in the figure of the consumer, the principle of fairness, and the standard of professional diligence—has been eroded by legal fragmentation, regulatory displacement, and the conceptual fading of the consumer as a distinct legal subject. Sector-specific instruments such as the Digital Services Act, Digital Markets Act, and Artificial Intelligence Act have sidelined horizontal protections and reframed individuals as “users” or “natural persons,” weakening the normative and institutional coherence of consumer law. In response, the paper calls for a resilient revival through a Digital Fairness Act: a dedicated horizontal instrument addressing the structural and behavioural asymmetries of digital markets. Anchored in the concept of digital professional diligence, the proposed Digital Fairness Act would restore fairness and enforceability by targeting manipulative design, algorithmic opacity, and autonomy-distorting commercial practices. European Criminal Law European Fundamental Rights and Freedoms European Law Legal Aspects of Computing European Economic Law Digital Ethics Legal Frameworks and Human Rights in Workplace Regulation Introduction: Unfairness First The term “digital fairness” is gaining traction in EU political and academic discourse. Yet, its contours remain ambiguous, functioning more as a rhetorical device than a coherent legal standard. In his 2024 mission letter, Commissioner Michael McGrath was explicitly tasked with giving legal shape to this notion by developing a Digital Fairness Act (“DFA”). While the DFA does not currently exist as a formal legislative proposal, the political mandate—combined with the European Commission’s recent Fitness Check on EU consumer law—signals institutional readiness for reform. This paper responds directly to that moment by offering a normative blueprint for what such an instrument could entail. It proposes a renewed legal foundation for fairness in the digital economy grounded in consumer protection principles, engaging with the legal design of the DFA as a much-needed regulatory reset. Particular attention is paid to treating unfair commercial practices, where digital manipulation, opacity, and power asymmetries remain insufficiently addressed by the current EU consumer acquis. The decision to narrow the scope of the DFA to commercial practices is not merely a matter of legislative pragmatism. It is a matter of conceptual clarity and legal necessity. First, unfair commercial practices remain the most direct and persistent source of consumer harm in digital markets. These harms—from manipulative design patterns to personalized targeting and algorithmic nudging—are addressed only in fragmented and often insufficient ways across the broader consumer law framework. Second, focusing on commercial practices allows the DFA to restore and modernize the core protective function of the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (“UCPD”), which, despite its foundational role, is increasingly outpaced by technological developments. Rather than overextending the DFA into areas already governed by the Unfair Contract Terms Directive (“UCTD”) or the Consumer Rights Directive (“CRD”)—both of which raise distinct conceptual and enforcement challenges (see “The Silent Death or the Dawn of Revival?”)—a targeted approach offers the best chance of legal coherence, enforceability, and political feasibility. Third, this narrowed scope ensures complementarity rather than redundancy with other legal instruments. Many systemic problems affecting consumers today—such as algorithmic discrimination or platform lock-in—could be more effectively tackled through a modernized fairness standard applicable to all digital B2C commercial practices. Attempting to fold broader issues into the DFA would not only risk regulatory overlap but also dilute the normative force of the act. By concentrating on commercial practices, the DFA can serve as a horizontal safety net within the digital single market. This paper takes the position that such a safety net is urgently needed. While the rhetoric of digital fairness gains ground, the legal foundations of fairness in EU consumer law have eroded. The DFA offers an opportunity not only to restore those foundations but to update them by introducing a tailored standard—digital professional diligence—responsive to structural, relational, and informational asymmetries. This concept is introduced here as a central doctrinal innovation and will be elaborated further. The structure of the article is as follows: Section"Diagnosis: The Silent Death of EU Consumer Law" explains how EU consumer law is being eroded by fragmented digital legislation and outdated legal frameworks, which fail to address new forms of digital manipulation and vulnerability, ultimately marginalising the consumer as a distinct legal subject in the digital era.; Section "Implications: Fragility of Consumer Protection" explains how the fragmentation of EU consumer law and the rise of digital market regulation have weakened its foundational principles, leading to structural legal gaps, economic distortions, and systemic social harms; Section "Expectations: A Resilient Revival of Consumer Law" argues that rather than relying on fragmented reforms, the EU must adopt a dedicated DFA—a comprehensive, forward-looking regulation rooted in digital professional diligence and designed to restore coherence, autonomy, and fairness in consumer protection across evolving digital markets and Section "Conclusions: Beyond the Horizon" argues that future consumer protection in digital markets requires a distinct, adaptive legal framework capable of evolving with technological change, rather than relying on static, omnibus-style legislation that risks becoming obsolete upon implementation.  Diagnosis: The Silent Death of EU Consumer Law A Holey Patchwork of Approaches The EU lawmaker has little choice but to adopt digital policy legislation—a stark admission of a regime compelled to innovate in the face of dynamic technological change. Digital markets, dominated by powerful online platforms, driven by sophisticated algorithms, and fuelled by relentless data exploitation, present challenges that EU consumer law struggles to address in its traditional form. Instruments such as the Digital Services Act (“DSA”) and the Digital Markets Act (“DMA”) have been introduced to regulate online platform responsibilities and to foster a level playing field. At the same time, the Artificial Intelligence Act (“AIA”) seeks to manage the risks associated with AI systems and their impacts on natural persons. Although these legislative measures are not designed as core consumer law, they ostensibly carry the mantle of safeguarding consumer rights in the digital age. This trend towards sector-specific regulation reflects a broader fragmentation, whereby no single legislative instrument assumes full responsibility for the consumer as a legal subject. This disaggregation results in normative dilution and regulatory overlap. However, their sector-specific focus casts a long shadow over the foundational principles of EU consumer law. By addressing distinct aspects of the digital ecosystem, these laws marginalize consumers’ vulnerabilities. The AIA, for instance, emphasizes transparency and accountability in AI systems; however, it fails to integrate these objectives with broader consumer protection imperatives such as fairness. This disjunction creates a legal scenery where digital policy legislation governs isolated dimensions of the digital economy, leaving the comprehensive protection of consumer rights in abeyance. This disjointed regime limits the reach of individual instruments and undermines the possibility of a unified digital fairness framework—one in which consumer protection is treated as a systemic concern rather than a residual benefit. Similarly, while the DMA and the DSA seek to enhance market competition and enforce platform accountability, they often regard consumers as secondary beneficiaries rather than central stakeholders. These instruments risk relegating consumer welfare to a secondary consideration by focusing predominantly on market regulation and structural remedies. They also perpetuate existing regulatory gaps and create new ones, arising from fragmented protective measures in new digital legislation, a lack of laws, or inadequate legislation (Helberger et al., 2021b). Nevertheless, a fully cohesive system capable of addressing every infringement of consumer rights in the digital realm is not feasible. Admittedly, a significant source of regulatory gaps is fast-paced technological innovation; new technologies and business models frequently emerge that fall outside the scope of existing legislation. Laws crafted in response to yesterday’s issues may be ill-equipped to confront the realities of tomorrow. Conversely, attributing these difficulties solely to the rapid evolution of the digital market would be an oversimplification. Legislative complexities further complicate the situation. Regulators may intentionally leave gaps, favouring a lighter touch in the hope that the market will self-regulate or due to concerns that overregulation might stifle innovation. At times, entrenched interests may resist certain legislative measures. Furthermore, the quality of legislative drafting must not be overlooked. Imprecise language, ambiguous terminology, and vaguely constructed provisions often result in multiple interpretations, compromising the consistent application of consumer protection standards. A recent example is Article 5 AIA, which prohibits certain practices that, while not explicitly consumer-focused, may arise in B2C contexts. To facilitate its interpretation, the European Commission issued 135-page guidelines—a notable effort to clarify a provision meant to prohibit certain practices in clear terms (European Commission, 2025). Yet even with such guidance, various enforcement mechanisms and differing interpretations across EU Member States further exacerbate these inefficiencies, widening the gaps in what should ideally be a coherent framework. The emergence of new legal issues does not automatically necessitate legislative reform. A well-grounded tradition in EU private law holds that existing general clauses can be reinterpreted to absorb new challenges, particularly in civil law systems that value continuity and legal certainty through judicial development (Twigg-Flesner, 2017). The UCPD’s general clause, akin to flexible standards in other legal instruments, such as Article 3(1) UCTD, exemplifies this adaptive potential. Yet, this approach presumes the continued existence of a clearly defined legal subject to protect—the consumer. As digital practices become more opaque, embedded, and systemic, traditional tools begin to fail, and so does the consumer’s legal visibility. The subject, around which fairness-based regulation was built, is at risk of conceptual erasure. While interpretive resilience remains essential in civil law systems, it is no longer sufficient in the face of persistent structural opacity and automation. What is needed is not a reinterpretation but a recalibration of consumer law’s foundations in light of digital asymmetries. These developments raise a more fundamental concern: as the regulatory net stretches and frays, the very figure it was designed to protect—the consumer—begins to disappear. The Vanishing Consumer In the digital age, the once-distinct figure of the consumer—an individual engaging in transactions for non-commercial purposes—is at risk of vanishing. This phenomenon reflects a broader transformation in legislation, where EU digital policy prioritizes general categories such as “users” (e.g., Article 25(3)(b) DSA; Article 71 AIA), “end users” (Article 2 point 20 of the DMA), “citizens” (e.g., Article 45(3) DSA), “recipients” (Article 26(1) DSA), and “natural persons” (e.g., Article 78 AIA). While these terms are inclusive and capture the multifaceted roles that individuals play in digital ecosystems, they dilute the specific and targeted protection measures that have historically underpinned EU consumer law. Traditionally, the concept of the consumer was delineated to capture the vulnerabilities and asymmetries present in B2C transactions. Consumer law was founded on the recognition that consumers, due to their position in the market, need a special regime designed to counterbalance the power of commercial entities. In contrast, recasting individuals as “users,” “citizens,” or “recipients” tends to obscure this dimension. The term “user” may suggest a degree of activity. Still, regulatory discourse often downplays the structural imbalances and manipulative design patterns in digital markets—emphasizing participation over protection. Similarly, “citizen” denotes rights and responsibilities within a civic context, shifting the regulatory discourse from market interactions to broader societal concerns. While this may signal an aspirational expansion of protection, it also risks displacing the economic specificity of consumer vulnerability. What emerges is a gradual erosion of the consumer’s legal identity. A hierarchy thus appears to be emerging: the “citizen” as an engaged rights-holder, the “user” as a functional participant, and the “consumer” as a market actor whose needs are increasingly framed in narrow, transactional terms. Likewise, referring to individuals as “recipients” diminishes the focus on their active vulnerability in transactional relationships, reducing their role to merely receiving goods or services without acknowledging the power imbalances that often prevail. Core characteristics of consumer transactions—such as exposure to unfair commercial practices—risk becoming invisible in regulatory and policy debates. This terminological shift carries far-reaching implications for the substance of EU consumer law. As the regulatory agenda shifts towards addressing systemic issues—such as digital platform governance and algorithmic accountability—consumer protection becomes less visible, less urgent, and more fractured. Instruments like the DSA, the DMA, and the AIA are crafted with a structural logic that emphasizes systemic fairness and innovation, as well as consumers’ everyday transactional dilemmas. The disintegration of the legal framework further exacerbates this problem. By disaggregating consumer protection into a series of sector-specific regulations, policymakers risk losing sight of the holistic mandate, thus signalling a broader deprioritization of consumer interests. The focus now shifts towards various regulatory imperatives that treat consumer protection as one element among many. This shift represents a fundamental departure from the ethos of EU consumer law, which has traditionally centred regulatory efforts around the consumer. The fragmentation of consumer laws and the dissolution of the consumer have led to the silent death of EU consumer law—a gradual weakening of a legal regime’s ability to fulfil its foundational objectives. It is characterized not by a single legislative or judicial failure but rather by the cumulative effect of reconfigured laws in which consumer rights are relegated to the status of ancillary benefits within broader market regulation. This transformation undermines the relevance of EU consumer law, making it increasingly reactive and disconnected from its original mandate. Once a clearly defined legal subject endowed with specific rights and protection tools, the consumer is increasingly subsumed into a nebulous category that fails to capture the transactional inequities of digital commerce. While this paper describes the vanishing consumer as a structural and conceptual trend, it does not suggest that the legal category of the consumer has been erased from EU law. Core legislative instruments such as the UCPD, the UCTD, and the CRD continue to rely on the traditional notion of the consumer as a legally protected subject in B2C transactions. Case law from the Court of Justice of the European Union likewise reaffirms consumer-specific protections in various domains. However, the concern raised here is not one of formal elimination but of functional marginalization: as digital regulation increasingly adopts broader categories—such as “users,” “natural persons,” or “recipients”—the distinct vulnerabilities addressed by consumer law risk being obscured. The consumer remains a legal category, but its normative centrality is weakened in a regulatory environment that fragments protection across policy fields without a coherent, horizontal standard. This evolving imbalance justifies a call for renewed legal clarity and structural reinforcement of the consumer’s role in digital governance. The weakening of the consumer’s regulatory visibility is not an isolated conceptual shift. It has practical consequences for the effectiveness of core consumer law instruments. The following Section turns to these doctrinal and functional limitations, focusing on the diminished capacity of the UCPD to address digital market realities. The Inadequacy of Traditional EU Consumer Frameworks in the Digital Age With the adoption of sweeping EU digital policy legislation, the limitations of traditional EU consumer law are becoming increasingly apparent, exposing a significant inadequacy in addressing the challenges posed by the digital economy. Before the digital era transformed market dynamics, the most effective instrument in consumer law was the horizontal UCPD, widely regarded as a safety net against unfair market behaviour. The European Commission has long lauded the UCPD as a mechanism that upholds a high common level of consumer protection, ensuring that unfair commercial practices across all sectors are monitored by complementing and filling gaps in other EU laws (European Commission, 2021). A safety net is established by combining a prohibition of unfair commercial practices (Article 5(1) UCPD) with the underlying general clause based on the principle of professional diligence (Article 5(2) UCPD). This combination covers all B2C commercial practices “not caught by other provisions of the UCPD (i.e., that are neither misleading nor aggressive under Articles 6–9 or listed in Annex I)” (European Commission, 2021). By establishing a general unfairness standard, the UCPD continues to provide a flexible response to emerging forms of unfair commercial practices in traditional B2C transactions. However, flexibility alone is insufficient in a regulatory environment characterized by technological opacity, structural power imbalances, and real-time consumer manipulation. Yet this flexibility, once a strength, now reveals its limits in an environment where commercial unfairness is automated, personalized, and designed into user interfaces. The expectations that the UCPD would remain a sufficient tool for filling legal gaps were high. However, this same safety net now appears inadequate for the digital age. Research and regulatory experience highlight significant difficulties in applying the UCPD to digital contexts (Helberger et al., 2021a, b). The Directive was primarily designed to address power asymmetries in traditional B2C transactions. While such asymmetries persist and have arguably deepened, in today’s digital economy, they manifest through complex, technology-driven dynamics. Digital platforms control market structures, curate the consumer experience, and manipulate the flow of information, thereby reshaping how power is exercised. This control fosters a digital market in which consumers are systematically disadvantaged—a structural vulnerability the UCPD was never intended to address. The UCPD remains grounded in the logic of counterbalancing power imbalances. However, its architecture presumes visible, traceable forms of unfairness in conventional B2C settings. Digital unfairness is built into algorithmic systems, embedded in interface design, and amplified by network effects—a structural shift the UCPD was never intended to address. Its effective application now depends on either heroic reinterpretation or structural reform. The new market phenomenon is best illustrated by the concepts of “digital asymmetry” and “digital vulnerability.” Digital asymmetry is defined as a situation of imbalance between traders and consumers concerning the knowledge and understanding of how a digital commercial practice functions (informational asymmetry), the imbalance in the commercial relationships that a digital environment creates and maintains (relational asymmetry), and structural differences in power that influence the autonomous decision-making process of the other party due to the control over data and/or a digital choice environment (structural asymmetry). Digital vulnerability refers to a universal state of susceptibility to exploiting differences in power within the trader-consumer relationship (Helberger et al., 2021a; Namysłowska, 2024a). Therefore, the average consumer benchmark—developed in the pre-digital era—is not conceptually viable. As digital environments systematically undermine consumer autonomy, the average consumer fiction cannot serve as the regulatory cornerstone (see also Esposito & Grochowski, 2022). Furthermore, algorithmic bias and personalized exploitation in commercial practices lead to harms that are either underregulated or not captured in current legal provisions. As digital vulnerability intensifies alongside the exploitation of behavioural biases, the traditional framework demonstrates its inability to protect consumers from sophisticated, multi-dimensional harm. The professional diligence standard under Article 5(2) UCPD, while originally designed to be a catch-all tool, struggles to address harms associated with algorithmic discrimination, personalized manipulation, or real-time interface design. As Helberger et al. (2021b) point out, these harms often fall beneath the visibility threshold of regulators and consumers alike. The digital age has broadened the scope of consumer harm, extending it beyond economic dimensions to encompass psychological, physical, and societal aspects. These harms include mental health issues, lack of sleep, and obsessive–compulsive symptoms, like compulsive buying among young adults; links to attention deficits and shorter attention spans, including risk factors for suicide and self-harm; information overload and excessive sensorial stimuli throughout the day; risks to neurodevelopment, learning, and memory; increased risk of early neurodegeneration; and not spending time being physically active, moving, being outside, shutting down, or relaxing (European Parliament, 2023). This reality renders traditional legal responses insufficient. Such harm resists simple economic valuation, yet it shapes the autonomy and well-being consumer law aims to protect. Another illustrative example of the inadequacy of the current legal framework is the interaction between the UCPD and EU digital policy legislation. Article 25 DSA introduces provisions aimed at curbing deceptive or manipulative online interfaces—tactics embedded in online interfaces that distort consumers’ abilities to make free and informed decisions. However, these measures are limited to aspects of choice architecture and do not address addictive behavioural design. Moreover, their applicability is confined to providers of online platforms, leaving other services unregulated. Even more problematic is Article 25(2) DSA, which explicitly excludes the possibility of applying the prohibition in paragraph 1 to practices covered by the UCPD or the General Data Protection Regulation (“GDPR”). As clarified in Articles 2(4)(f) and (g), as well as in Recital 10, the DSA is intended to operate “without prejudice” to consumer and data protection law. While this aims to prevent normative overlap, it risks creating interpretive gaps where manipulative practices are not explicitly addressed by existing instruments (Raue, 2023). The reliance on ex-post enforcement mechanisms also poses serious difficulties in the digital context. Digital commercial practices are often subtle, obscured by algorithmic decision-making and real-time adaptations, making detecting and quantifying consumer harm exceedingly difficult. Numerous studies confirm that consumers remain unaware of how interface architecture and algorithmic recommendations shape their choices. The European Commission found that 97% of leading websites contained at least one dark pattern, often unnoticed by users (European Commission, 2022). Empirical studies demonstrate that deceptive design patterns systematically reduce user awareness and erode autonomy, even among digital natives (e.g., Rossi et al., 2024). When harm is identified, however, proving that, for instance, algorithmic recommendations or personalized content led to a harmful decision requires technical expertise and access to data, which is a burden that disproportionately falls on consumers and often exceeds even the capabilities of national authorities. Consequently, although a new legal issue does not automatically require a legislative overhaul, the intersection of rapid technological advancements and emerging market trends offers a strong justification for reassessing and, where necessary, reforming the legal frameworks (see Durovic, 2024). The ability of the UCPD to effectively safeguard consumers against unfair commercial practices has been fundamentally compromised by business practices associated with novel business models, technological design strategies, and unprecedented scale and scope. As such, targeted regulatory measures are crucial—a responsibility for which the traditional framework is becoming increasingly inadequate. In particular, the structural and behavioural dynamics of digital markets make traditional enforcement reactive and slow, especially when unfairness is not incidental but embedded in the architecture of digital choice. In short, the current consumer law framework—however flexible in theory—has reached its functional and conceptual limits in addressing embedded, systematized forms of digital unfairness. These limitations are not confined to legal doctrine; they produce ripple effects across economic behaviour, social trust, and institutional legitimacy. The following Section explores these broader implications. Implications: Fragility of Consumer Protection The Erosion of Foundational Principles of Consumer Law EU consumer law has long been grounded in two core principles: the pursuit of a high level of consumer protection and the assurance of a properly functioning internal market. These twin pillars, enshrined in Article 114 TFEU and major legislative acts like Article 1 UCPD, were designed to protect consumers from exploitation while fostering cross-border trade. Together, they once formed a unified logic: empowering consumers would deepen integration, and integration would bolster consumer trust. Today, however, the structure underpinning this framework of consumer protection is persistently deteriorating. This erosion stems primarily from the above-mentioned regulatory fragmentation and the growing dominance of sector-specific legislation. Though these measures aim at competition enforcement or systemic risk management, they frequently undermine coherence and reduce the clarity of consumer protection standards. The UCPD serves as a prime example by explicitly articulating its dual purpose: to ensure a high level of consumer protection and to contribute to the proper functioning of the internal market. The underlying premise was that harmonized rules across Member States would empower consumers and create the trust essential for market integration. However, this vision is no longer being sustained by EU legislative practice. What was once a cohesive framework anchored in shared principles is now fractured, with consumer interests dispersed across overlapping and occasionally conflicting regimes. The rise of EU digital policy instruments such as the DSA and the DMA exemplifies this shift. These measures are ambitious in scope but rely heavily on models of self-regulation, co-regulation, and structural remedies—approaches that privilege system-wide governance over individual protection. This signals a departure from the consumer-centric ethos of earlier EU law. Moreover, fragmentation across Member States undermines the harmonization needed for a truly functioning internal market. Divergent regulatory approaches create inconsistencies that weaken consumer protection and diminish legal certainty for consumers and businesses. The original mandate—to protect individuals from exploitation while enabling market integration—becomes entangled in competing priorities. As regulatory agendas pivot towards digital innovation and economic competitiveness, the figure of the consumer as a rights holder is increasingly displaced by that of the consumer as a data point—subject to quantification, profiling, and algorithmic management. This disjointed evolution of consumer law reflects a broader regulatory ambivalence. While digital markets require strong horizontal safeguards, EU legislation increasingly defaults to vertical, sector-based interventions. As a result, the protective function of consumer law is exercised through scattered proxies—data protection clauses, transparency obligations, or fairness provisions with no consistent interpretive centre. The implications of this erosion are structural and normative. When consumer protection is reframed as a secondary concern within a broader digital strategy, the foundational integrity of EU consumer law is compromised. The balance shifts from a dual focus on protection and market integration to a technocratic emphasis on innovation, compliance, and market efficiency. To restore that balance, a deliberate legislative recalibration is needed—one that re-centres consumer protection as a foundational principle in EU digital governance. Economic and Social Costs In the digital age, the fragility of consumer protection has profound economic and social repercussions that extend far beyond the traditional vulnerabilities once confined to specific demographic groups. In conventional markets, regulatory gaps primarily affected the elderly, those with lower educational attainment, or individuals with limited financial resources. Today’s digital realm renders every consumer digitally vulnerable due to the pervasive influence of data-driven commercial practices and the asymmetries they create (see more in “The Inadequacy of Traditional EU Consumer Frameworks in the Digital Age”). Economically, the erosion of adequate consumer protection triggers systemic distortions that jeopardize the proper functioning of digital markets. When consumers lose trust in the fairness of digital transactions, widespread disengagement may ensue. Such withdrawal places ethical businesses at a competitive disadvantage, as firms engaging in exploitative practices can achieve short-term gains through manipulation and cost-cutting measures. This dynamic risks initiating a race to the bottom, where the pursuit of economic efficiency undermines regulatory compliance and consumer trust. In the long run, this dynamic undermines market integrity and suppresses innovation, as exploitative practices entrench dominance and limit competition. Moreover, when consumers are disempowered by design—nudged, tricked, or manipulated through, for instance, dark patterns—the informational basis for competitive markets erodes. Markets cease to reflect informed choice and instead reward opacity and behavioural exploitation. Furthermore, where consent is shaped by market dominance and an imbalance of power, as recognized by the Court of Justice of the European Union in Meta Platforms Inc and Others v Bundeskartellamt (2023), exploitative data practices may proliferate unchecked. In that judgment, the Court acknowledged that the assessment of free consent under the GDPR cannot be detached from competition law considerations, especially where market power enables a platform to impose exploitative terms. This reasoning underscores the Court’s growing recognition of relational and structural asymmetries in digital markets, reinforcing the need for consumer law instruments to address similar imbalances explicitly. The social costs of inadequate consumer protection in the digital age are equally significant. Digital players possess an unparalleled capacity to exploit universal cognitive, emotional, and relational vulnerabilities. Tracking systems collect vast amounts of behavioural data, enabling companies to target individuals at their most vulnerable moments—whether they are fatigued, stressed, or experiencing financial hardship. Even those who perceive themselves as digitally literate are not immune; advanced algorithms are designed to circumvent human decision-making, exploiting cognitive biases and employing manipulative design patterns (e.g., Rossi et al., 2024). This is not merely an issue of harm but a systemic disempowerment (Zuboff, 2019). As digital environments increasingly shape the terms of attention, emotion, and decision-making, the capacity for autonomous consumer choice erodes in ways that legal frameworks struggle to address. For instance, dark patterns in app interfaces obstruct the cancellation of subscriptions or obscure the actual cost of services, preying on human impulsivity and cognitive overload. This systematic manipulation undermines the traditional assumptions of reasonably well-informed, reasonably observant and circumspect consumer and fosters a broader sense of disempowerment and alienation. The Court of Justice of the European Union has recently acknowledged in Compass Banca SpA v AGCM (2024) that constraints such as cognitive biases may impair an individual’s decision-making capacity. However, it also held that not every risk of bias automatically distorts consumer behaviour in the sense required by Article 5(2) UCPD. This nuanced position illustrates the growing awareness of psychological manipulation in commercial practices and the current legal threshold that may exclude systemic design-based harm unless it crosses into material distortion. This ambiguity highlights the urgency of updating the general unfairness test for digital realities. When consumers frequently encounter deceptive and manipulative practices, their autonomy erodes, and trust in digital markets—as well as the institutions that claim to regulate them—diminishes. This cumulative erosion of trust extends beyond individual dissatisfaction. When consumers view marketplaces and regulatory frameworks as fundamentally unfair, widespread disillusionment and disenfranchisement may arise, undermining the legitimacy of democratic governance and destabilizing society cohesion. This creates a feedback loop in which consumer alienation feeds distrust, and distrust reduces regulatory effectiveness. Failing to address digital vulnerabilities risks entrenching systemic inequalities adequately. Although the challenges posed by the digital world are universal, certain groups—such as consumers with limited digital literacy, persons with disabilities, or residents of rural areas with restricted access to high-speed internet—bear a disproportionate burden. These consumers often lack the tools or knowledge to manage complex digital ecosystems, making them particularly susceptible to exploitation. Moreover, the technological superiority of certain market players over others often results in the deployment of unfair commercial practices that target not only digitally vulnerable consumers but also weaker businesses. Without targeted interventions, the digital divide may widen further, perpetuating inequality cycles and undermining inclusive access to fair markets. Addressing these economic and social costs requires a paradigm shift. Policymakers must acknowledge that the digital sphere influences every consumer and that traditional frameworks—which assume a baseline of rationality and informed decision-making—are ill-equipped to handle the opacity, asymmetry, and psychological manipulation endemic to digital ecosystems. Closing regulatory loopholes is critical not only for future-proofing consumer protection but also for restoring the legitimacy of the law and upholding the core societal values of fairness and transparency (Micklitz, 2022; Scheuerer, 2021). It is also striking that while consumer law protections remain fragmented, the EU has taken more decisive steps to protect weaker business actors. Regulations such as the Platform-to-Business Regulation and the Data Act grant considerable protections to small and medium-sized enterprises operating under platform dependence. This asymmetry of regulatory concern reveals a paradox: while entrepreneurs increasingly benefit from fairness-based protections, consumers face systemic power imbalances without a comparable horizontal safety net. The cumulative effect of these normative, economic, and social tensions is apparent: the current regulatory model is no longer fit for purpose. Fragmented instruments, reactive enforcement, and narrow notions of consumer autonomy cannot keep pace with the systemic risks posed by digital market architectures. Yet, this decline is not irreversible. Beneath the apparent disintegration of EU consumer law lie the conceptual resources and political conditions necessary for renewal. The following Section considers how a resilient revival may be achieved—not through incremental updates alone, but through a structural reimagining of fairness in the digital economy, grounded in a renewed normative commitment to consumer protection. Expectations: A Resilient Revival of Consumer Law The Silent Death or the Dawn of Revival? The silent death of EU consumer law is not a result of a sudden legislative failure or overt political rejection. It reflects a broader systemic failure to adapt to the realities of the digital world. However, within this seemingly inevitable decline lies the potential for a transformative revival. By addressing the root causes of digital asymmetry, the paradigm shift proposed here can restore legal coherence and reinvigorate fairness as a normative anchor. Incremental upgrades to sector-specific digital legislation are no longer sufficient. Although these digital policy measures may seem better equipped to tackle unfair digital commercial practices that evade the grasp of traditional consumer law, they often overlook the complex interplay between sector-specific regulations and the broader consumer acquis. This oversight creates regulatory gaps and overlaps, weakening consumer protection’s coherence and overall effectiveness (see more on regulatory gaps, e.g., Cauffman & Goanta, 2021; Veale & Borgesius, 2021). When digital policy becomes the de facto mechanism for consumer protection, the conceptual and protective integrity of consumer law is diluted. As a result, the logic of consumer protection is gradually subordinated to technological governance frameworks, fragmenting its identity and effectiveness. A dual-track strategy is therefore needed, one that reinforces EU digital policy legislation while simultaneously restoring consumer protection as an autonomous regulatory domain. Ideally, this reimagined framework would provide a layered, multi-dimensional protective architecture, with each layer supporting consumer autonomy. In this vision, EU digital policy legislation should function as a complementary layer—not a substitute that eclipses the general clause of consumer law. Provisions like Article 25(2) DSA, which preclude overlap with existing consumer law, underscore the risk of digital policy marginalizing horizontal protections. To avoid this, a clear delineation of competences is needed—one that preserves consumer law’s distinctive role in regulating market asymmetries, design-based manipulation, and behavioural exploitation. Reflections on the future of EU consumer law are intertwined with the initiatives of Michael McGrath, the recently appointed Commissioner for Democracy, Justice, the Rule of Law, and Consumer Protection. Tasked by Ursula von der Leyen with developing a Digital Fairness Act (European Commission, 2024b), McGrath now faces an unprecedented opportunity to redefine EU consumer protection through a digital lens. The conception of the DFA reveals the possibility of establishing a new legal paradigm that aligns political ambition with regulatory capacity. If successful, it would transform “digital fairness” from an aspirational buzzword into a legal principle capable of guiding legislative design and enforcement. This raises questions about the scope and structure of the reforms required. An early step in this direction came with the European Commission’s initiative “Digital fairness – fitness check on EU consumer law,” which reviewed three key directives: the UCPD, the UCTD, and the CRD. One central question in the public consultation was whether the professional diligence standard should be further clarified in the digital context. The 2024 report acknowledges that “(t)he existing EU legal framework cannot be considered sufficiently effective in tackling current and emerging consumer harms” (European Commission, 2024a). While the DFA is not explicitly referenced, the report’s concerns—such as combating the most harmful practices, preventing regulatory fragmentation, and ensuring coherent application of EU consumer law alongside other EU legislation—call for structural reform (see more Mišćenić & Tereszkiewicz, 2024). A pragmatic next step could involve a targeted, procedural instrument: an “Omnibus 2 Directive” proposed under the umbrella of the DFA. Like its predecessor—the so-called Omnibus Directive (Directive (EU) 2019/2161), which amended four key EU consumer law instruments in 2019—the UCPD, UCTD, CRD, and the Price Indication Directive—this initiative would introduce amendments to existing directives, particularly the UCPD, to address digital-specific unfair practices such as interface manipulation and algorithmic personalization (European Commission, 2021). The appeal of such an approach lies in its legislative efficiency and political feasibility. Targeted amendments often face fewer procedural hurdles than wholly new legislative acts. However, procedural convenience must not compromise normative ambition. Recent scholarship has started to address some of the regulatory gaps identified in the Digital Fairness Fitness Check. Busch and Twigg-Flesner (2024) outline a roadmap for regulating subscriptions under the proposed DFA, highlighting persistent issues such as cancellation barriers, automatic renewals, and the role of intermediaries in the “subscription stack.” Their proposal reinforces the need for a layered regulatory response and demonstrates how targeted reforms can be integrated into a broader framework of digital fairness. Still, to avoid the pitfalls of past reforms, the DFA must articulate a comprehensive vision. The Omnibus Directive—though lauded as a step towards modernization—ultimately fell short. Its reforms were fragmented and reactive, adding transparency obligations without addressing structural asymmetries. It failed to confront the logic of digital exploitation and did not develop a unifying framework. Without a forward-looking conceptual model, consumer protection risks being locked in a cycle of legislative catch-up. The logic of piecemeal intervention also threatens to entrench inequality. By focusing on narrow forms of vulnerability, it underestimates the systemic nature of digital manipulation and its disproportionate effect on marginalized consumers. The result is a patchwork of protections unevenly distributed across platforms, sectors, and Member States. A fragmented regime is ill-suited to capture the transversal nature of algorithmic targeting, opaque data flows, and cross-platform interface manipulation—each transcends the boundaries of sector-specific legislation. Moreover, the reactive nature of the Omnibus approach neglects power. While it addressed transparency gaps, it did not meaningfully challenge the concentration of power among dominant platforms. Regulation focused on transparency without addressing structural asymmetries is normatively shallow and strategically ineffective. Some legal scholars have explored amending the UCPD itself (Namysłowska, 2024a). However, the scale and complexity of digital market failures justify the creation of a separate legal instrument. The DFA should serve as the digital twin of the UCPD—drawing on its horizontal structure, general clause logic, and blacklist mechanism while extending this architecture to address asymmetries specific to algorithmically mediated markets. This structural parallelism does not imply duplication but rather conceptual alignment adapted to digital. The DFA must engage directly with digital asymmetry, online vulnerability, and the erosion of consumer autonomy. Harms such as psychological manipulation, emotional distress, and algorithmic exclusion fall beyond the economic logic of earlier directives. A recalibrated framework is, therefore, required. The DFA could represent a pivotal moment for EU consumer law—if it is not watered down by incrementalism. A genuine conceptual shift is needed: a legal redefinition of consumer protection in algorithmically curated environments. The DFA must integrate insights from neighbouring fields—data protection, competition, platform governance—while reaffirming consumer protection’s foundational concern: the defence of autonomy in asymmetrical markets. A Blueprint for Resilient Consumer Protection Conceptually inspired by the UCPD, the proposed DFA must be designed as a legal framework that directly addresses unfair digital commercial practices. Its primary objective is to equip EU consumer law with a resilient and forward-looking architecture—one that can remain effective in the face of accelerating technological innovation. In this context, resilience means regulatory adaptability: the ability to anticipate and respond to new forms of digital harm rather than merely react. Notably, the proposals outlined here deliberately exclude areas governed by the UCTD or CRD, which were already reviewed in the Commission’s Fitness Check. Instead, the DFA concentrates narrowly on commercial practices that distort consumer autonomy and decision-making. By establishing a uniform baseline for digital fairness, the DFA would restore coherence and fill the legal voids that currently undermine consumer protection across the Union. A cornerstone of the DFA is its role as a horizontal safety net—a legal framework that applies across sectors and platforms. Central to this design is the general clause prohibiting digital unfairness, defined as practices that breach the principle of digital professional diligence and materially distort the autonomous decision-making of consumers (see “A Lifeline for Consumers”). Although it builds on the familiar logic of professional diligence from traditional B2C law, the digital version must account for design-based manipulation, algorithmic opacity, and structural power asymmetries. Digital professional diligence requires that traders refrain from exploiting digital asymmetries or consumers. It extends beyond intention and appearance to address the embedded architecture of consumer manipulation. Moreover, the scope of digital unfairness should include breaches of legal obligations that sustain fairness in digital commerce, even where those obligations are found outside consumer law (e.g., transparency requirements, accessibility rules, and data protection duties). By doing so, the DFA recognizes that the digital economy functions through layered legal systems and that unfairness often emerges through their intersection. In pursuit of uniformity and legal certainty, the DFA should be enacted as a regulation. Unlike directives, which require national transposition and may lead to fragmentation, the regulation would be directly applicable and uniformly enforceable, reducing compliance costs for businesses and offering clearer rights to consumers. It would also allow regulators to act more confidently and coordinate across Member States. The DFA must be horizontally applicable across all B2C relationships, regardless of sector. A sector-specific approach is insufficient in a digital ecosystem where platforms mediate everything from product search to content consumption. Activities in one domain (such as advertising) directly influence behaviour in another (such as e-commerce or subscriptions). A horizontal framework ensures legal consistency and closes loopholes arising from siloed regulation. The logic is simple: the interface, not the industry, defines the consumer’s experience. The DFA should mirror the three-tiered structure of the UCPD. At its core, it must establish a general prohibition on unfair digital commercial practices, clarified by a general clause—a familiar solution within continental legal systems that enables a legislative act to function as a safety net (Sack, 1985). By defining unfair conduct, such a clause sets a behavioural standard anchored in extra-legal principles such as good morals, good faith, fair practices, and professional diligence (e.g., Grundmann, 2006). In the context of the DFA, this general clause should expressly incorporate the notion of digital professional diligence. In addition to this overarching prohibition, the DFA should contain targeted provisions addressing specific harmful practices, including influencer marketing, dark patterns, algorithmic discrimination, and deceptive personalization (European Commission, 2024a). Finally, the inclusion of a list of digital commercial practices deemed unfair in all circumstances—modelled on the UCPD’s black list—would, if precisely formulated, provide clarity for market participants by minimizing interpretive ambiguity (Keirsbilck, 2024) and serving as a strong deterrent to manipulative digital practices. Such clarity aligns with the European Commission’s commitment to simplifying EU consumer law (Draghi, 2024; European Commission, 2024a). Combined with a general clause, these provisions would signal regulatory resolve and a principled vision. The DFA must be deeply rooted in the core consumer law principles of the EU, especially fairness and transparency—but these concepts must evolve. In the context of data-driven commerce, traditional transparency becomes insufficient. It must be replaced with digital transparency accomplished through regulatory frameworks (regulatory transparency), achieved by ensuring clarity of commercial practices (operational transparency) and realized through the employment of clear and accessible language (formal transparency) (Namysłowska, 2024b). Similarly, unfairness must be refined into digital unfairness: a standard that addresses not only deceptive acts but also structurally manipulative systems that degrade autonomy. This reflects a broader epistemic shift: from isolated misconduct to systemic exploitation, from individual rationality to environmental vulnerability. In short, the DFA must be a normative statement and a regulatory tool. It should articulate what fairness means in the digital economy and provide regulators and courts with the doctrinal infrastructure to enforce it. A Lifeline for Consumers The DFA, grounded on a general prohibition of unfair digital commercial practices and clarified by a general clause, has the potential to operationalize digital fairness as a regulatory principle. At its core, the DFA envisions a horizontal safety net for the digital marketplace—one in which fairness is not merely a guiding value but a legally enforceable standard. One of the key strengths of a horizontal framework lies in its broad scope and structural flexibility. It ensures that a baseline level of protection applies across all sectors, even where digital commercial practices are novel or elude existing regulatory definitions. This structural resilience is especially critical in a regulatory landscape where digital innovation routinely outpaces legal clarity. For instance, certain emerging systems may fall outside the scope of the AI Act (Article 5) or evade gatekeeper designations under the DMA (Article 3) yet still pose substantial risks to consumer autonomy. These gaps illustrate the necessity for a catch-all instrument capable of addressing unforeseen and hybrid practices (Helberger et al., 2021b; Veale & Borgesius, 2021). A horizontal standard fills such gaps and functions as an early-warning mechanism, guiding future adjustments to sectoral legislation. The cornerstone of this architecture is the general clause, which must encompass the concept of digital professional diligence—a reconfiguration of a traditional legal standard to meet the specific demands of the digital age. As defined in the proposed framework: “Digital professional diligence means not exploiting digital asymmetry and/or digital vulnerability by a trader towards consumers” (see more in Namysłowska, 2024a). This definition is concise and comprehensive, facilitating legal clarity while reflecting the normative foundations of Article 2(h) UCPD (professional diligence). By explicitly naming digital asymmetry and digital vulnerability, the clause provides an analytical framework suited to the complexity of digital environments. It also answers criticisms of legal ambiguity in recent regulatory acts (see Savin, 2022), offering regulators and courts a precise standard to assess conduct. To reinforce the general clause, the DFA should integrate tested doctrinal tools from European legal traditions, particularly the concept of “breach of law” (germ. Rechtsbruch), which links unfairness to violations of legal norms designed to protect market participants. This model, developed over decades in German jurisprudence and later codified into the UWG in 2008, distinguishes between value-neutral and value-related legal provisions—allowing courts to determine when a breach reflects a broader unfair market strategy (Köhler, 2021). Polish legal developments provide a similar anchor: the Act on Combating Unfair Competition (1993) introduced language tying unfairness to actions “contrary to the law or good morals,” offering legal clarity in a transitioning economy (Jasińska, 2019). These examples highlight the value of grounding digital unfairness in doctrinally mature concepts capable of anchoring new norms without sacrificing legal certainty. Integrating the breach of law concept into the DFA also facilitates doctrinal convergence between consumer protection, competition law, and data governance. This enables a more systemic assessment of harm and reinforces the coherence of EU internal market law. The proposed provision would read: “A digital commercial practice shall be unfair if a) it is contrary to the requirements of digital professional diligence and/or the law, and b) it materially distorts or is likely to materially distort a consumer’s autonomous decision-making.” This formulation broadens the scope of protection by shifting the focus from economic behaviour to decisional autonomy, thereby capturing harms that are non-financial but no less profound. It aligns with the broader jurisprudence on digital vulnerability and reflects a deeper understanding of consumer harm in data-driven markets (Jabłonowska et al., 2018; Namysłowska, 2024a). While some may argue that strict reliance on digital professional diligence could render the concept of a breach of law redundant, this view overlooks the full complexity of digital commerce. The Court of Justice of the European Union has already recognized in Pereničová and Perenič (2012) that different EU consumer protection instruments may be applied complementary, even under a full harmonization. In that case, the Court held that assessing a commercial practice under the UCPD did not preclude a parallel evaluation under the UCTD, provided that the objectives and legal bases of the instruments are respected (Helberger et al., 2021a; Keirsbilck, 2013). This compatibility logic is doctrinally important for the proposed DFA. It suggests that introducing a new general clause of digital unfairness—grounded in digital professional diligence—need not conflict with existing rules. Instead, it can function as a horizontal safety net, reinforcing but not duplicating adjacent legal standards. The overarching aim should be to identify a shared core across the various fairness tests within EU legislation (Helberger et al., 2021a). Significant academic debate has addressed the inclusion of the concept of breach of law in consumer protection frameworks. Some scholars have suggested that its incorporation may conflict with the UCPD’s principle of full harmonization, potentially exceeding the limits of a unified legal standard (e.g., Köhler, 2021). However, this concern loses force when the breach of law standard is embedded within a new EU legal act such as the DFA. In sum, the combined use of digital professional diligence and breach of law offers a dual foundation for evaluating digital unfairness. This framework clarifies existing uncertainties and empowers regulators to act pre-emptively against emerging forms of manipulation and structural asymmetry. The proposed clause represents more than a legal formula. It reflects a conceptual shift in how consumer law understands vulnerability, choice, and market power. Its adoption would strengthen the legal position of consumers in digital contexts marked by opacity and automation. It would also reinforce the principle that consumer protection is not ancillary to innovation but integral to sustainable and legitimate digital markets. The vision of a DFA outlined above rests on a clear normative imperative: to move beyond outdated assumptions and confront manipulation at the level where it now operates—architecture, data, and design. But if the need for legal reform is conceptually evident, its success depends on institutional readiness to break with the logic of incrementalism. The question, then, is not whether reform is needed but whether the EU is prepared to lead it. The final section reflects on the risks of delay, the limits of reactive lawmaking, and the urgent need for a forward-facing legal architecture capable of protecting consumers in the digital environment. Conclusions: Beyond the Horizon The pace of technological transformation renders the future of unfair digital commercial practices inherently unpredictable. What is certain, is that these practices will grow in sophistication, subtlety, and reach. Merely adapting legacy frameworks will not suffice. What is required is a regulatory architecture that is both structurally resilient and conceptually agile—capable of anticipating the contours of digital unfairness before they fully emerge. Against this backdrop, the DFA cannot afford to replicate the logic of the Omnibus Directive. Although comprehensive in appearance, such an approach risks becoming obsolete on arrival. One-size-fits-all instruments are ill-equipped to respond to the accelerating pace and increasing complexity of digital manipulation. The real danger lies not in legislative inaction but in adopting frameworks that are too static to capture moving targets. As the digital marketplace evolves, the Omnibus model risks locking EU consumer law into reactive governance—perpetually behind the curve of innovation. This recognition forms the basis of the proposal advanced in this paper: a standalone legislative instrument that reimagines the architecture of consumer protection. The DFA should serve as the digital twin of the UCPD—drawing on its horizontal structure and general clause logic while extending its scope to address asymmetries specific to algorithmically mediated markets. Such a framework would secure continuity with the consumer acquis while equipping the EU with the tools needed to confront manipulation embedded in data architectures, platform design, and behavioural targeting. At its core, this proposal is not merely technical. It is a normative commitment to consumer-centric regulation. The DFA prioritizes the autonomy and decisional integrity of individuals in digital markets. It recognizes that consumer protection must evolve from regulating isolated transactions to regulating the environments in which choices are shaped. It repositions fairness not as a residual concern but as the structuring principle of digital legality. Minor concessions to incrementalism may prove expedient—such as limiting reforms to additional transparency obligations or an expanded black list—but they must not come at the expense of conceptual clarity or normative ambition. Any legislative instrument that aspires to regulate digital markets must include a general clause defining digital unfairness, grounded in a standard of digital professional diligence and recognition of pervasive vulnerability. Without such a clause, EU consumer law will lack the granularity required to identify manipulation, the coherence to coordinate enforcement, and the legitimacy to protect rights effectively in digital space. In 2006, the UCPD was rightly hailed as “one of the most significant pieces of legislation to emanate from Brussels” (Howells et al., 2006). The DFA should earn the same distinction. But this will demand more than a policy gesture. It will require legislative resolve, political vision, and institutional courage. This paper has described a silent death. It now concludes with a call for a resilient revival—not as an act of nostalgia but as a necessary recalibration of consumer protection in the algorithmic age. The question is no longer whether the legal framework must evolve. The only question that remains is whether the European Union is willing to lead that evolution.
